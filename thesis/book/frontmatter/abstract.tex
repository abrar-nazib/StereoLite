\chapter*{Abstract}

Mobile robots, drones, and augmented-reality devices need dense depth in
real time, and they must compute it within a power budget of only a few
watts. Binocular stereo matching supplies this depth passively from two
ordinary cameras, but the most accurate stereo networks reach their
accuracy with foundation-scale backbones of hundreds of millions of
parameters, a computational cost embedded hardware cannot pay. Closing
that gap is the central problem this thesis addresses, together with a
practical second constraint: the compact stereo rigs these platforms
carry are rarely perfectly rectified, so a network built for them must
also tolerate small camera misalignments rather than assume the ideal
geometry lab benchmarks provide.

This thesis presents StereoLite, a 2.96 million parameter stereo
matching network designed to meet both constraints. Rather than
searching a full four-dimensional cost volume the way heavier, more
accurate networks do, StereoLite estimates disparity for each small
image tile and repeatedly corrects that estimate with a learned
recurrent update, an approach that keeps computation and memory small
while recovering most of the accuracy a full search would give.
Concretely, a group-wise correlation volume at one sixteenth resolution
initializes each tile's disparity and confidence; a dedicated
left-image context encoder then feeds convolutional GRU updates that
refine every tile across three resolutions; a narrow-band geometry
encoding volume adds a long-range correction through a learned gate
that acts only where it helps; and plane-equation rendering with
learned convex upsampling recovers a sharp, full-resolution disparity
map from the coarse tile grid.

Trained only on synthetic SceneFlow data, the network reaches 0.78 px
end-point error and 3.40 percent D1-all on the full FlyingThings3D test
set of 4,370 pairs. Without ever training on real images, it scores
10.9 percent D1-all zero-shot on the unseen Middlebury 2014 benchmark,
showing that its accuracy transfers to a real domain it never saw
during training. One forward pass at $384 \times 640$ takes 49.8 ms on
a laptop RTX 3050 GPU in half precision; after an inference-optimization
pass and eight-bit quantization, it runs in 36.3 ms, or 27.5 frames per
second, on the target Jetson Orin Nano, meeting the real-time target on
embedded hardware. The network also tolerates the imperfect
rectification a low-cost stereo rig produces: under a controlled
vertical misalignment sweep, end-point error rises only from 1.03 to
1.53 px across the sub-pixel to one-pixel range such a rig produces,
and on real indoor scenes captured with the project's own rig, the
predicted disparity back-projects through the calibrated geometry into
metric colored point clouds. The training code, the evaluation drivers,
and the trained checkpoint are archived so that every reported number
can be reproduced.
