\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Overview}
Depth perception is a prerequisite for a machine that must act in
three-dimensional space: a mobile robot avoids obstacles, a drone holds
its position, and an augmented-reality headset anchors virtual content
only if it knows how far away each surface lies. Binocular stereo
recovers that depth passively from two inexpensive image sensors, but
the dense correspondence it requires is computationally demanding, and
the deep networks that now solve it most accurately are far too large
and too slow for the embedded accelerators these platforms carry. This
thesis designs, trains, and deploys a compact stereo matching network
that closes that gap: it delivers dense disparity, and through it metric
three-dimensional reconstruction, within the parameter, latency, and
memory budget of edge hardware. This chapter states the background and
motivation, the objectives, the research methodology, and the plan and
organization of the work.

\section{Background and Motivation}
Depth is not an optional cue but the quantity on which the safety and
usefulness of spatially acting systems depend. The sensing options
differ sharply in cost and character. Scanning LiDAR measures time of
flight along emitted laser beams and delivers accurate but sparse points
at a price and power draw that exclude small platforms.
Structured-light and time-of-flight cameras emit infrared patterns or
pulses and recover dense depth indoors, but their emitters limit range,
fail in sunlight, and interfere with one another when several devices
share a space. Among the passive ways to recover depth, binocular stereo
is attractive for embedded systems because it emits nothing, draws little
power, works indoors and outdoors alike, and uses two inexpensive image
sensors rather than a laser scanner. Its cost is computational: the
correspondence between the two views must be solved for every pixel, and
that computation is precisely what this thesis makes affordable.

Binocular stereo recovers depth from the horizontal shift of a scene
point between two horizontally separated views. That shift, the
disparity, is inversely proportional to depth through a calibrated
pinhole relation developed in Chapter 2. A dense disparity map therefore
yields a dense depth map, which downstream perception and reconstruction
consume directly, and computing that disparity map accurately and quickly
is the task this thesis addresses.

The applications that motivate this thesis share a common trait: they
carry a small power and compute budget and demand results in real time,
but they stress depth differently. A mobile robot needs reliable obstacle
depth at a few meters and tolerates modest resolution; a drone needs the
same at higher frame rates because its closing speeds are larger; an
augmented-reality headset needs sharp depth boundaries so that virtual
content occludes correctly against real edges; and an embedded sensing
rig needs metric accuracy so that the recovered geometry can be measured
and reconstructed. A single network that serves all four must therefore
balance accuracy, boundary sharpness, latency, and memory rather than
excel at any one of them, and that balance is the design objective of
this thesis.

Deep networks have transformed the accuracy of stereo matching. The most
accurate systems now pair a foundation-scale monocular prior with an
iterative refinement core; FoundationStereo, for example, reports 0.34
pixel end-point error on the SceneFlow benchmark with a backbone of
roughly 340 million parameters \cite{wen2025foundationstereo}. However,
this accuracy is bought with a computational cost that embedded hardware
cannot pay. The edge accelerators found on mobile robots and drones
deliver a few tera-operations per second, carry a few gigabytes of memory,
and operate within a power envelope of a few watts to a few tens of watts.
A network with hundreds of millions of parameters and hundreds of
milliseconds of latency does not fit that budget. The problem this thesis
addresses is therefore not accuracy alone but the joint objective of
accuracy, latency, and memory: a stereo network that is accurate enough to
be useful, small enough to fit in on-device memory, and fast enough to run
in real time on an edge accelerator. Figure \ref{fig:edge_gap} places the
published literature on this trade-off and marks the region this thesis
targets.

\section{Objectives}
The objectives of this thesis are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item To design a computationally efficient stereo matching pipeline that
leverages AI-based disparity refinement to enhance depth estimation on
resource-limited platforms.
\item To design an architecture that can withstand camera imperfections
in terms of rectification.
\end{enumerate}

The scope of this thesis is passive rectified binocular stereo, trained by
supervision on synthetic data with dense ground truth. The design targets
a compact network of a few million parameters, evaluated both in domain
and zero-shot on an unseen real domain, with its inference optimized for
the edge latency envelope and qualified on real camera pairs. The
recovered disparity is carried through to metric three-dimensional
reconstruction: real scenes captured by the project's stereo rig are
back-projected into colored point clouds through the calibrated
geometry, which is the reconstruction the thesis title refers to. Active
sensing, self-supervised training, monocular depth estimation, and the
production of a deployment software development kit are outside the scope.
The evidence for the second objective, tolerance to imperfect
rectification, is twofold and reported in Chapter 8: a controlled
perturbation study on synthetic data with known ground truth, and a
real-data demonstration on a low-cost stereo rig whose factory
rectification is imperfect, on which the model retains low error.

A natural question is why this thesis designs a new compact network rather
than compressing an existing large one. Two reasons motivate the choice.
First, the accuracy of the foundation-scale systems rests substantially on
their monocular priors and their scale, neither of which survives
aggressive compression, so compressing them tends to discard the very
component that makes them accurate. Second, the tile-hypothesis and
recurrent-refinement families were designed for efficiency from the
outset and offer a cleaner starting point for an edge budget.

\section{Research Methodology}
The work follows an evidence-gated design methodology: every
architectural choice is admitted only after it wins a controlled
comparison, and every trained model is judged on accuracy, latency, and
memory jointly rather than on any single metric. Design proceeds by
single-knob ablation on a fixed harness of one hundred training pairs, so
that competing variants are compared under identical data, seed, and
compute; the surviving configuration is then trained on the full dataset
on cloud GPUs, evaluated in domain, zero-shot on an unseen real
benchmark, and under controlled rectification perturbation, and finally
optimized and measured on the target edge device. The steps, elaborated
in Chapters 3, 7, and 8, are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item study of the stereo matching literature and its efficient and
edge-deployable families;
\item definition of the design requirements and the edge deployment
envelope;
\item architecture design by controlled single-knob ablation on a fixed
100-pair harness;
\item full-dataset training of the selected configuration on cloud GPUs;
\item evaluation in domain, zero-shot on an unseen real domain, and under
rectification perturbation;
\item optimization of the network for edge inference and on-device
latency measurement.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Summary}
This chapter introduced the problem the thesis addresses: dense, accurate
stereo depth on hardware that offers only a few tera-operations per
second, a few gigabytes of memory, and a few watts. It motivated passive
binocular stereo over active sensing for embedded platforms and located
the gap between the accuracy of foundation-scale stereo networks and the
budget of edge accelerators. Two objectives were stated: a
computationally efficient stereo matching pipeline with AI-based
disparity refinement for resource-limited platforms, and an architecture
that withstands camera imperfections in rectification. The research
methodology was outlined as a pipeline of literature study, controlled
single-knob ablation, full-dataset cloud training, in-domain and
zero-shot and robustness evaluation, and on-device measurement. The
chapter closed with the project work plan and the organization of the
remaining eight chapters.
