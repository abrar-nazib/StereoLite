\chapter{Design Methodology and Implementation}

\section{Introduction}
Chapter 3 fixed the architecture of StereoLite; this chapter states how
that architecture was turned into a trained, optimized, and measurable
system. It details the training objective and the dataset protocol,
records the selection of hyperparameters, hardware, and software, and
describes the optimization pass that brought the network within its edge
latency budget. It then explains how the design process was reviewed and
fed back on itself, lays out the simulation and experimental setup, walks
through the principle of operation of the deployed pipeline from
rectified image pair to metric point cloud, and defines the measurement
protocols under which every number of Chapter 8 was obtained. The results
themselves are reported and discussed in Chapter 8; this chapter confines
itself to the methodology and implementation that produced them.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Detailed Design Methodology}
The design methodology has two implementation-level components: the
objective the network is trained to minimize, and the data and
optimization protocol under which that objective is applied. Each is
detailed in turn.

\subsection{Training Objective}
The network is trained with the multi-scale supervised objective of
\eqref{eq:loss}. A pixel counts as valid when its ground-truth disparity
is finite and lies between 0 and 320 pixels. Each lower-scale prediction
is resized to full resolution and multiplied by its scale factor before
the comparison. The objective weights an L1 term at every scale most
heavily at full resolution, adds a matching L1 term on the
geometry-volume output, and augments the full-resolution prediction with
a Sobel gradient-consistency term, a threshold hinge stack, a KITTI-style
D1-relative hinge, an edge-aware smoothness term, and a gated
slant-supervision term that trains the plane slopes only where they are
trustworthy. The slant term pairs with plane rendering: it makes the
slopes accurate enough for the rendering step to sharpen boundaries. The
composition of the objective is itself an experimental result rather
than a guess. The loss ablation of Chapter 8 tested nine formulations
and found that a plain L1 under-serves the outlier metrics, that hinge
terms on a single threshold overfit that threshold, and that the
threshold stack combined with the D1 hinge was the only formulation to
win the end-point error and the outlier rates together; the production
objective descends from that winner, with the gradient term retained for
its sub-pixel benefit and the smoothness term kept at a deliberately
small weight so it regularizes without blurring.

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L} \;=\;& 1.00\,\mathcal{L}_{1}(d_{\mathrm{full}})
 + 0.50\,\mathcal{L}_{1}(d_{1/2})
 + 0.30\,\mathcal{L}_{1}(d_{1/4})
 + 0.20\,\mathcal{L}_{1}(d_{1/8})
 + 0.10\,\mathcal{L}_{1}(d_{1/16})\\
&+ 0.15\,\mathcal{L}_{1}(d_{\mathrm{gev}})
 + 0.50\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{grad}}
 + 0.20\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{thr}}
 + 0.20\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{D1}}
 + 0.02\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{smooth}}
 + 0.30\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slant}}
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_1$ is the masked mean absolute disparity error,
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{grad}}$ a Sobel gradient-consistency term,
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{thr}}$ a hinge over the error thresholds
$\{0.5, 1, 2, 3\}$ pixels, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{D1}}$ the D1-relative
hinge, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{smooth}}$ an edge-aware smoothness term, and
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slant}}$ the gated slant supervision.

\subsection{Datasets and Training Protocol}
The model is trained on the full SceneFlow finalpass dataset. The
canonical split contains 35,454 training pairs, drawn from the
FlyingThings3D training set together with Monkaa and Driving, and the
FlyingThings3D test set of 4,370 pairs is held out for evaluation; only
FlyingThings3D provides an official test split, so the test set is drawn
from it alone. Training samples random 384 by 640 windows that are
co-located in both views from the native 960 by 540 frames, which
preserves the native disparity magnitudes. Chapter 8 shows that this
native-crop protocol substantially outperforms the legacy protocol of
downscaling the whole frame. Three augmentations from the OpenStereo
recipe are applied: asymmetric color jitter, right-image-only random
erasing, and random scaling. Optimization uses AdamW with a OneCycle
schedule over 60,000 steps at a peak learning rate of $8 \times 10^{-4}$,
a batch of 32, and bfloat16 mixed precision, on a single A100 GPU. The
input is normalized to the unit interval at the model boundary.
Validation uses a fixed 400-pair subset of the test set at the native
960 by 540 axis, evaluated every thousand steps, with a resized-axis
measurement logged alongside. Table \ref{tab:train_config} lists the
configuration.

\section{Selection of Parameters, Apparatus and Components}
The network's hyperparameters were not chosen in a single pass; each
load-bearing value is the survivor of a controlled study reported in
Chapter 8, and the design-evidence audit of Chapter 3 maps every choice
to its justification. The principal architectural parameters are the
truncated YOLO26s backbone retained through its first seven blocks, a
32-channel recurrent hidden state, a 48-channel refinement head, eight
correlation groups over 24 disparity hypotheses at one sixteenth
resolution, a local correlation half-range of two pixels, a refinement
schedule of two, three, and three iterations across the three scales, and
a geometry encoding volume narrowed to a band of plus or minus 16 around
the tile disparity. The training-side parameters are those of Table
\ref{tab:train_config}. The complete configuration record of the trained
model, verbatim from its checkpoint, is given in Table
\ref{tab:arch_config} of Appendix A and is not duplicated here.

The apparatus divides into compute and sensing. The model is implemented
in PyTorch 2.11 with CUDA 12.8 on Python 3.10. Training runs on a single
cloud A100 GPU with 80 gigabytes of memory; ablations and evaluations run
on a cloud T4, whose modest cost permits a controlled study after every
design change; and the local latency benchmark runs on an RTX 3050 laptop
GPU with roughly 4 gigabytes of memory, which stands in for the
memory-constrained end of the deployment spectrum during development. The
intended deployment target is the Jetson Orin Nano class of edge
accelerator, on which the optimized model was measured directly; the
on-device result is reported in Chapter 8. On the sensing side,
real-camera pairs come from a calibrated low-cost binocular rig that
streams a 2560 by 720 side-by-side image, split into a rectified 1280 by
720 pair per frame. This rig supplies the real-data qualification of the
trained model: the zero-shot qualitative pairs, the real-camera agreement
protocol of Section \ref{sec:testing_protocols}, and the scenes
reconstructed in three dimensions in Chapter 8.

\section{Optimization and Configuration Selection}
Reaching the latency target required an optimization pass over the
network's inference graph, treated as an engineering task in its own
right. Six changes were applied. The cost-volume construction loops were
rewritten to use padded zero-copy views instead of allocating and zeroing
a feature map for every disparity bin. The grid-sample calls were reduced
from 48 to eight by batching the lookups and caching the sampling grids.
Static channels of the recurrent input, which are re-convolved every
iteration, were hoisted once per scale, following the precomputed-context
trick of RAFT-Stereo \cite{lipson2021raftstereo}. The gate convolutions
and the four output heads, which share inputs, were fused. Dead
inference-path code was removed. Finally, the geometry volume was narrowed
to a band around the tile disparity. Each change was proven equivalent
before adoption, either bitwise or through a matched A/B comparison, and
together they cut the full-precision latency by a factor of 1.74. The pass
also surfaced three export blockers for eight-bit deployment, namely the
unfold operation in convex upsampling, group normalization on some
accelerator toolchains, and three-dimensional convolution on neural
processing units, each of which has a documented replacement.
Table \ref{tab:efficiency_findings} summarizes the changes.

\section{Review and Feedback}
The implementation advanced under an explicit review-and-feedback
discipline rather than by unchecked iteration. Every proposed design
change, whether an architectural block, a loss term, or an input-protocol
variant, was gated by a controlled ablation on the matched harness of
Chapter 3 before it could enter the production configuration; a change
that failed to win its comparison was not adopted, however plausible its
motivation. Every run, winning or losing, was logged with its full
configuration and metrics to a master experiments log, so that the
evidence trail behind each decision can be audited and no comparison
depends on memory. Intermediate results, including training curves,
qualitative panels, and ablation tables, were reviewed with the
supervisor at regular intervals, and the feedback from those reviews
redirected the work more than once, most visibly toward the
cross-dataset evaluation gate that now follows every training run.
Failed variants were documented rather than discarded: the rejected
mechanisms and anti-synergistic compositions reported in Chapter 8 are
retained precisely because they constrain future work as firmly as the
adopted ones justify the present design.

\section{Simulation and Experimental Setup}
All experiments run against the setup described here; the results
themselves are reported in Chapter 8. Three datasets are used. The
SceneFlow finalpass dataset provides the synthetic training corpus and
the in-domain test bed, under the split and protocol of Table
\ref{tab:train_config}: 35,454 training pairs and the held-out
FlyingThings3D test set of 4,370 pairs, with a fixed 400-pair subset
serving as the validation and perturbation-sweep set. The Middlebury 2014
benchmark, 23 perfect-set scenes of real indoor imagery that the model
never sees during training, serves as the zero-shot cross-domain test.
The third source is the project's physical stereo rig, the calibrated
2560 by 720 side-by-side camera of Section 7.3, which supplies real
rectified pairs with no ground truth for qualitative evaluation, teacher
agreement, and three-dimensional reconstruction.

The evaluations run as scripted drivers on cloud GPUs, one driver per
experiment: full-test-set evaluation, zero-shot Middlebury 2014
evaluation under a protocol identical for our model and the published
reference checkpoints, and the rectification-robustness sweep. Each
driver records its configuration and metrics to a metadata file alongside
its outputs, and Appendix A names the specific scripts so that every
reported number can be reproduced. Cross-domain evaluation on a cloud T4
costs minutes and cents per run, which is what makes the
after-every-training-run evaluation gate of Section 7.5 practical.
Accuracy in every experiment is measured with the standard stereo metrics
defined in \eqref{eq:metrics}, averaged per image and then over images;
the measurement protocols for latency, rectification robustness, and
real-camera agreement are specified in Section
\ref{sec:testing_protocols}.

\section{Principle of Operation}
In deployment the system operates as a fixed feed-forward pipeline from a
rectified image pair to a metric depth map, exercising the blocks of
Chapter 3 in sequence. A rectified left and right pair enters at the
inference resolution of 384 by 640 pixels, normalized to the unit
interval. The shared encoder processes both views in a single batched
invocation and emits matching features at one quarter, one eighth, and
one sixteenth resolution, while the separate context encoder reads the
left image alone and produces the context maps that feed the recurrent
state.

Matching begins at the coarsest scale. The tile initialization module
builds the group-wise correlation volume of \eqref{eq:gwc} at one
sixteenth resolution, regularizes it with a small three-dimensional
network, and reads off the initial disparity and confidence through the
soft-argmax of \eqref{eq:softargmax_init}; the plane slopes start at zero
and the hidden state starts from the coarsest context map. The recurrent
refinement then runs its fixed schedule of two iterations at one
sixteenth, three at one eighth, and three at one quarter resolution. At
each iteration the right features are warped by the current disparity, a
local correlation is evaluated over five offsets, and the convolutional
gated recurrent unit of \eqref{eq:convgru} updates the hidden state, from
which gated residuals adjust the disparity, the plane slopes, and the
confidence. Between scales, the plane-aware propagation of
\eqref{eq:plane_prop} carries the tile state to the finer grid along its
local slanted-surface hypothesis rather than by plain interpolation.

At one quarter resolution the narrow-band geometry encoding volume
produces its independent proposal per \eqref{eq:gev_expect}, and the
fail-soft gate of \eqref{eq:failsoft} blends that proposal into the
recurrent estimate only where the learned weight favors it, correcting
regions the tile state resolved poorly without disturbing those it
resolved well. The refined quarter-resolution state is then rendered
from its tile planes with an edge gate and upsampled to full resolution
by the two learned convex-combination stages, each output subpixel a
softmax-weighted mixture of its three-by-three coarse neighborhood. The
result is a dense full-resolution disparity map.

Disparity becomes depth through the calibrated relations of
\eqref{eq:backprojection}: depth follows $Z = fB/d$, and each valid pixel
optionally back-projects to a metric three-dimensional point in the
camera frame, colored by its left-image pixel. On the project's rig the
calibrated focal length and baseline scale to the inference resolution by
the width ratio, so the point cloud is metric without further tuning.
This single forward pass, image pair in, disparity and optionally a
colored point cloud out, is the operation measured throughout Chapter 8.

\section{Testing and Measurement}
\label{sec:testing_protocols}
Four measurement protocols govern the experiments of Chapter 8; they are
specified here so that every reported number has a stated procedure, and
the numbers themselves appear only in that chapter.

The first protocol measures latency and memory. Latency is measured at
batch one and 384 by 640 resolution, with warm-up passes preceding the
timed passes, and memory is recorded as the peak inference working set;
the device is stated for every measurement. On the RTX 3050 laptop GPU
the model is timed in eager PyTorch in both full and half precision,
without compiler assistance, so the figures reflect the unoptimized
serving path. On the deployment target, the Jetson Orin Nano, the
optimized model is exported to an eight-bit TensorRT engine and measured
directly on the device, which is the figure of merit for the real-time
claim.

The second protocol tests tolerance to imperfect rectification under
controlled conditions. The trained model is evaluated on the fixed
400-pair FlyingThings3D subset at the native axis while the right image
is shifted vertically by a controlled offset and the disparity ground
truth is left unchanged, so the offset emulates a residual vertical
misalignment between the views. The sweep covers offsets from zero
through sub-pixel values to several pixels, and the standard metrics are
reported at each offset.

The third protocol corroborates the synthetic sweep on physical data.
The model is run on 997 rectified pairs from the project's stereo rig,
whose factory rectification leaves a residual vertical misalignment, and
its disparity is compared against the pseudo-ground-truth produced by the
FoundationStereo teacher on the same pairs. Because both the model and
the teacher observe the same misaligned input, this protocol measures
agreement with a strong reference rather than absolute error, and it is
interpreted as corroborating, not replacing, the controlled sweep.

The fourth is the dual-axis evaluation convention. Every in-domain
evaluation is reported on the native 960 by 540 axis, which preserves the
true disparity magnitudes, with a resized-axis measurement at 640 by 384
logged alongside for comparability with the legacy protocol; input-protocol
comparisons evaluate every arm on both axes so that a gain on one axis
cannot hide a loss on the other. Zero-shot Middlebury 2014 evaluation
follows a single fixed protocol applied identically to our model and to
the public reference checkpoints: inference at 384 by 640, ground truth
scaled to the inference width, and disparities above 192 pixels masked,
with per-scene metrics macro-averaged over scenes.

\section{Summary}
This chapter recorded the path from the architecture of Chapter 3 to a
trained and deployable system. The network is trained with a multi-scale
composite objective whose formulation was itself selected by ablation,
under a native-crop SceneFlow protocol with the OpenStereo augmentation
triplet, a OneCycle schedule, and cloud A100 hardware. Hyperparameters,
apparatus, and software were selected on documented evidence, with the
full configuration recorded in Appendix A, and an equivalence-proven
optimization pass brought the inference graph within the edge latency
budget. The design process was gated by controlled ablations, logged to a
master experiments record, and reviewed regularly with the supervisor.
The experimental setup spans a synthetic training and test corpus, an
unseen real benchmark, and a physical stereo rig, and four measurement
protocols, covering latency, rectification perturbation, real-camera
agreement, and dual-axis evaluation, define how every number of Chapter 8
was obtained.
