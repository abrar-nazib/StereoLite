\chapter{Thesis Management and Finance}

\section{Introduction}
A thesis is also a project, and this chapter records how it was managed
and what it cost. The work ran from February to July 2026 and was carried
out almost entirely on rented cloud compute, which kept the hardware cost
low and made the spending both small and fully itemizable. Section 6.2
describes the management of the work and its time frame across seven
phases; Section 6.3 states the overall budget; Section 6.4 breaks the
budget into its hardware and its cloud and software components; Section
6.5 summarizes.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Management of the Thesis}
The project was organized as a sequence of phases with explicit outputs,
so that each phase produced an artifact the next phase consumed: the
literature study produced the design candidates, the ablation campaign
produced the validated architecture, the production run produced the
trained model, and the evaluation produced the results of Chapter 8.
Progress was gated on evidence rather than on the calendar: the
production training run was launched only after the ablation campaign
had settled the architecture, and the writing consolidated only results
that had passed their evaluation gates.

\section{Component/Software Budget}
The budget of Table \ref{tab:expenses} divides into a hardware component
and a cloud and software component.

The hardware component consists of a single purchased item, the stereo
camera rig at approximately 60 USD, which served both as the source of
the real-data experiments of Chapter 8 and as the model of the deployed
sensor. This small hardware bill is itself part of the economic case for
the approach, which rests on the cost of the deployed sensor rather than
on the cost of development. A passive stereo rig requires two commodity
image sensors and an embedded accelerator, a bill of materials on the
order of a few tens of dollars, whereas a comparable active-depth or
LiDAR unit costs several times as much and adds an emitter.

The cloud and software component covers everything else: the production
training run on a cloud A100 at approximately 10 to 15 USD for about one
day, the ablation sweeps on T4 and L40S instances at approximately 15 to
25 USD across many short runs, the evaluation runs on T4 at
approximately 5 USD in total with each run costing a fraction of a
dollar, and storage and miscellaneous cloud services at approximately 10
USD. The software stack itself, the deep-learning framework and the
supporting tooling of Chapter 7, is open source and incurred no license
cost. Because the model runs on the device, no recurring cloud-inference
cost is incurred in deployment. The combination of a low sensor cost and
on-device inference makes the approach attractive for price-sensitive
robotics and embedded products.

\section{Summary}
This chapter recorded the management and finance of the thesis. The work
ran from February to July 2026 in seven phases, from paper collection
through design, ablation, production training, and evaluation to
writing, with each phase gated on the output of the one before it and
the calendar extents shown in the work plan of Chapter 1. The total
spend was approximately 100 to 115 USD, dominated by cloud compute, with
the stereo camera rig at 60 USD as the only hardware purchase and an
open-source software stack at no license cost. The deployed sensor
itself carries a bill of materials on the order of a few tens of dollars
and incurs no recurring cloud-inference cost, which is the economic case
for the approach.
