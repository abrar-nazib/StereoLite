\chapter{Design of StereoLite}

\section{Introduction}
This chapter presents the design of StereoLite, the compact stereo
matching network developed in this thesis: the problem and research
questions, the hardware envelope and design criteria, the controlled
exploration methodology that produced the final architecture, the
system model, the conceptual design and its literature ancestry, the
record of preliminary design decisions, and a block-by-block detailed
design with all governing equations. The chapter closes with the
performance indices used to assess the design; training procedure and
inference optimization are treated in Chapter 7, and all experimental
results in Chapter 8.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Problem Statements}
Stereo matching takes a pair of rectified images and produces a dense
disparity map, from which metric depth follows directly through the
calibrated relation of \eqref{eq:depth_from_disparity}. This thesis
addresses that task under a hardware budget rather than an accuracy
budget alone; the requirements motivating this budget are reviewed in
Chapter 2, and the resulting hard envelope is stated in the next
section. These requirements frame four research questions: whether a
network within a few million parameters can reach competitive
in-domain accuracy on synthetic data; whether it generalizes zero-shot
to an unseen real domain; whether it meets the real-time latency
target on the intended device class; and whether it tolerates the
small vertical misalignments of imperfect rectification, the second
objective of this thesis. The design assumes rectified binocular input, a
maximum disparity of 192 pixels at the inference resolution, and
training on synthetic data with dense ground truth.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Design Requirements and Requisites}
\label{sec:design_requirements}
The design requirements follow from the class of edge accelerators
found on mobile robots and drones. Together they form a hard envelope:
a candidate design that violates any one of them is rejected regardless
of accuracy:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Under 3 million parameters.} The network must be
    compact enough for on-device memory and storage; parameter count is
    also the first-order proxy for a deployed model's download and
    update footprint.
  \item \textbf{Real time on the target edge accelerator.} The
    deployment target, an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano, delivers 20 to 40
    tera-operations per second of eight-bit throughput
    \cite{nvidia_orin_nano}, and the network must complete a forward pass
    within a live camera stream's frame budget on it.
  \item \textbf{Under 200 megabytes of inference working set at the
    deployment precision.} Peak activation memory in the eight-bit
    deployment path must stay small enough to coexist with the rest of a
    robot's software stack on a shared-memory edge board; a
    higher-precision development GPU may use more.
  \item \textbf{INT8-exportable operators.} Every operator on the
    inference path must quantize cleanly to eight-bit integer
    arithmetic, since the accelerators in this class reach their rated
    throughput only under integer execution.
\end{itemize}

\FloatBarrier
\section{Design Criteria}
Within the envelope of the previous section, candidate designs are
judged on balance rather than on any single number. Every design
decision is evaluated on the full eight-metric stereo set: end-point
error, root-mean-square error, median absolute error, the bad-0.5,
bad-1, bad-2, and bad-3 rates, and the D1-all rate, together with
inference latency and peak memory; these quantities are defined
formally in Section \ref{sec:design_assessment}. A variant that
improves one metric while degrading the others, or that buys accuracy
with latency the envelope cannot afford, is not accepted. Because
in-domain gains can mask a loss of generalization, a cross-dataset
zero-shot evaluation is mandatory after every training run. All
comparisons are made under equal conditions: the same training pairs,
random seed, and step budget, so a measured difference is
attributable to the design change alone.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Design Steps}
The architectural choices in this chapter were made under a controlled
matched-overfit methodology rather than intuition. Each candidate is
trained on a fixed set of SceneFlow pairs with a fixed seed,
deterministic convolution kernels, and an identical step budget and
evaluation protocol; all eight stereo metrics plus an
inference-latency benchmark are reported for every arm. Single knobs
are varied one at a time, the winning criterion is registered before a
run, and every candidate is checked against a held-out cross-dataset
evaluation, so an in-domain gain that fails to transfer is not
mistaken for progress. The results appear in Chapter 8; this section
states the protocol that produced them.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Modeling of Proposed System}
Figure \ref{fig:architecture} gives the complete pipeline: StereoLite is
a coarse-to-fine tile-hypothesis network with light recurrent
refinement. A shared feature encoder processes both views and a
separate left-image context encoder feeds the recurrent state; a
group-wise cost volume at the coarsest scale initializes a slanted tile
state; convolutional GRU updates refine the state across three scales
with plane-aware propagation between them; a narrow-band geometry
encoding volume corrects the estimate through a learned fail-soft gate;
and plane rendering with learned convex upsampling recovers the
full-resolution disparity.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Modeling of Conceptual Design}
Conceptually, the pipeline is an assembly of five stages, each of which
adapts a mechanism from the literature reviewed in Chapter 2 to the edge
budget. The matching
cost is a group-wise correlation volume in the manner of GwcNet
\cite{guo2019gwcnet}, restricted to a single coarse scale. The refinement
operator is a convolutional gated recurrent unit and the final upsampling
is a learned convex combination, both adapted from RAFT-Stereo
\cite{lipson2021raftstereo} but run for only a handful of iterations at
coarse resolution. A quarter-resolution geometry encoding volume, in the
spirit of IGEV-Stereo \cite{xu2023igev}, supplies a global disparity
proposal that a learned gate blends into the recurrent estimate. Slanted
tile planes, following the HITNet paradigm \cite{tankovich2021hitnet},
carry a local surface hypothesis across scales so disparity remains
sub-pixel without rebuilding a cost volume at every level. A shared
feature encoder feeds the matching path, and a separate left-image
context encoder feeds the recurrent state. The detailed design of each
stage follows in Section \ref{sec:detailed_design}.

\section{Detailed Design}
\label{sec:detailed_design}
This section works through each pipeline block in the order data flows
through it, together with the governing equations for each stage.

\subsection{Shared Feature Encoder and Context Encoder}
The matching network is a truncated YOLO26s backbone whose first seven
blocks are retained, producing features at one quarter, one eighth, and
one sixteenth resolution with 128, 256, and 256 channels. The choice of
a detection backbone is deliberate: its features are pretrained to
localize objects across scales, which is close to what stereo matching
needs from an encoder, and the encoder ablation of Chapter 8 confirms
that this backbone beats both a hand-built GhostConv stack and its own
smaller sibling on every accuracy metric at a two-millisecond cost. The
detection neck and head above the one-sixteenth stage are removed at
construction, not merely left unused, because dead blocks would waste
parameters and forward compute. The two views are concatenated along
the batch dimension and pass through a single shared invocation, so
both images are processed with identical weights. A separate context
encoder processes the left image alone: a strided convolution is
followed by two GhostConv blocks with squeeze-and-excitation, and the
result is projected to a 32-channel quarter-resolution context map that
is pooled to the coarser scales. Matching evidence and recurrent
context are kept in separate streams for a specific reason: if the
matching features also served as context, the recurrent unit would not
be able to separate appearance from correspondence. The RAFT-Stereo
lineage \cite{lipson2021raftstereo} established that a dedicated context
path improves refinement, and the same separation is adopted here.

\subsection{Tile Initialization and Cost Volume}
The tile initialization module builds a group-wise correlation volume at
one sixteenth resolution. For each of eight feature groups and each
integer disparity, the matching score is the mean channel-wise product
between the left features and the horizontally shifted right features,
as given in \eqref{eq:gwc}. A small three-dimensional convolutional
network with an eight, sixteen, sixteen, one channel schedule
regularizes the volume, and a softmax along the disparity axis yields a
probability distribution. The initial disparity and confidence come
from the soft-argmax and the distribution peak, as in
\eqref{eq:softargmax_init}. The plane slopes are initialized to zero and
the hidden feature is replaced by the coarsest context map. The 24
hypotheses at this scale span roughly 0 to 368 full-resolution pixels.

\begin{equation}
C(g,d,y,x) \;=\; \frac{1}{C/G}\sum_{c=1}^{C/G}
  f_{L}^{\,g,c}(y,x)\; f_{R}^{\,g,c}(y,\,x-d)
\label{eq:gwc}
\end{equation}
where $G = 8$ is the number of feature groups, $C$ the channel count,
$d \in \{0, \dots, 23\}$ the integer disparity hypotheses at one sixteenth
resolution, and $f_{L}^{\,g,c}$, $f_{R}^{\,g,c}$ the $c$-th channel of
group $g$ of the left and right feature maps.

\begin{equation}
d_{0} \;=\; \sum_{d} p(d)\, d, \qquad
c_{0} \;=\; \max_{d}\, p(d)
\label{eq:softargmax_init}
\end{equation}
where $p(d)$ is the softmax of the regularized cost volume along the
disparity dimension, $d_0$ the expected disparity, and $c_0$ the peak
probability that serves as an initial confidence.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{fig_3_3_tile_init_preview}
\caption{\textbf{Tile-state initialization at 1/16 resolution.} A group-wise correlation volume (8 groups, 24 hypotheses) is regularized by a small 3-D CNN; soft-argmax and the distribution maximum yield the initial disparity and confidence, and the context map replaces the zero hidden state.}
\label{fig:tile_init}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Recurrent Tile Refinement}
Separate refinement modules refine the tile state at one sixteenth, one
eighth, and one quarter resolution; their weights are not shared,
because the matching-feature channel counts differ across scales. At
each iteration the right feature map is warped toward the left view by
the current disparity, and a differentiable local correlation is
evaluated over five offsets within a half-range of two pixels. The
recurrent input assembles the left features, the warped right features,
the current state, the local correlation, and the context, as in
\eqref{eq:gru_input}. A convolutional gated recurrent unit then updates
the persistent hidden state through the gate equations of
\eqref{eq:convgru}. A two-layer head with 48 hidden channels predicts
residuals for disparity, both plane slopes, and confidence, and a
second learned gate emits four independent sigmoid weights that scale
each residual. A softplus constrains disparity to remain non-negative.
The schedule runs two iterations at one sixteenth, three at one eighth,
and three at one quarter, for eight learned updates in total.

\begin{equation}
x \;=\; \bigl[\, f_{L},\; \mathcal{W}(f_{R};\,d),\; d,\; s_{x},\; s_{y},\;
  c,\; \mathrm{corr},\; \mathrm{ctx} \,\bigr]
\label{eq:gru_input}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{W}(f_{R}; d)$ denotes the right features warped by the
current disparity, $\mathrm{corr}$ the five-offset local correlation, and
$\mathrm{ctx}$ the scale-matched context map.

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
z &= \sigma\!\bigl(\mathrm{Conv}([h,\,x])\bigr), &
r &= \sigma\!\bigl(\mathrm{Conv}([h,\,x])\bigr),\\
q &= \tanh\!\bigl(\mathrm{Conv}([r \odot h,\,x])\bigr), &
h' &= (1-z) \odot h + z \odot q,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:convgru}
\end{equation}
where $h$ is the 32-channel persistent hidden state, $z$ and $r$ the
update and reset gates, $q$ the candidate state, and $\odot$ the
element-wise product.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_3_4_refinement_preview}
\caption{\textbf{One recurrent refinement iteration.} The right features are warped by the current disparity, a five-offset local correlation is evaluated, and the assembled input updates the persistent hidden state through a convolutional GRU; four sigmoid gates control the residual updates of disparity, both slopes, and confidence.}
\label{fig:refinement}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Plane-Aware Cross-Scale Propagation}
Between adjacent scales, the propagation module resamples the disparity,
slopes, hidden feature, and confidence to the finer grid. Disparity is
multiplied by two because its pixel unit changes with resolution. Unlike
plain bilinear interpolation, the propagated disparity also uses the
local plane slopes to place the four child pixels around each parent
tile, as given in \eqref{eq:plane_prop}. This tile-plane component, in
the manner of HITNet \cite{tankovich2021hitnet}, preserves a local
slanted-surface hypothesis while moving to a finer grid; sub-pixel
structure is retained without rebuilding a cost volume at every level.

\begin{equation}
d_{\mathrm{child}} \;=\; 2\,\mathcal{B}(d_{\mathrm{parent}})
  \;+\; 2\,s_{x}\,\Delta x \;+\; 2\,s_{y}\,\Delta y,
\qquad \Delta x, \Delta y \in \{-0.25,\, +0.25\}
\label{eq:plane_prop}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{B}$ is bilinear resampling of the parent disparity, $s_x$
and $s_y$ the local plane slopes, and $\Delta x, \Delta y \in \{-0.25,
+0.25\}$ the child offsets; the factor of two rescales the disparity to
the finer grid.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_3_5_plane_prop_preview}
\caption{\textbf{Plane-aware propagation between scales.} Each parent tile spawns four children whose disparities follow the local plane defined by the slopes, per \eqref{eq:plane_prop}, instead of plain bilinear interpolation.}
\label{fig:plane_prop}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Geometry Encoding Volume and Fail-Soft Fusion}
The geometry encoding volume adds an independent global proposal at one
quarter resolution, in the spirit of IGEV-Stereo \cite{xu2023igev}. In
the trained configuration it is narrow: rather than the full 64
disparity planes of the base design, it samples 33 hypotheses in a band
of plus or minus 16 around the current tile disparity. Chapter 8
validates this change as accuracy-neutral, and it also removes roughly
a quarter of the model's multiply-accumulate operations. The band is
less restrictive than it sounds: 16 quarter-resolution planes correspond
to 64 full-resolution pixels on either side of the current estimate,
and since the coarse initialization has already localized the
disparity, the band's job is correction, not search. The full-range
volume spends most of its computation scoring hypotheses the tile state
has already ruled out, so removing them costs nothing. The volume is
regularized by three three-dimensional convolutions with 16 hidden
channels, and the softmax distribution yields the expected disparity, a
confidence, and a projected geometry feature, as in
\eqref{eq:gev_expect}. The proposal is not substituted unconditionally:
a learned gate takes the context, the geometry feature, both
confidences, the absolute disagreement, and the current disparity, and
produces a scalar weight through \eqref{eq:failsoft}. The gate's final
bias is initialized to minus four, so the initial weight is
approximately 0.018 and training begins close to the stable recurrent
baseline; the network then learns where the geometry proposal helps.
The fail-soft design stops the global branch from corrupting regions
the recurrent path already resolves well.

\begin{equation}
d_{\mathrm{gev}} = \sum_{d} p(d)\, d, \qquad
c_{\mathrm{gev}} = \max_{d}\, p(d), \qquad
g_{\mathrm{gev}} = \phi\Bigl(\sum_{d} G(d)\, p(d)\Bigr)
\label{eq:gev_expect}
\end{equation}
where $p(d)$ is the softmax over the regularized quarter-resolution
volume, $G(d)$ the regularized three-dimensional geometry feature, and
$\phi$ a projection to a 16-channel two-dimensional expected-geometry map.

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
w &= \sigma\!\Bigl(F\bigl([\mathrm{ctx}_{4},\, g_{\mathrm{gev}},\, c,\,
  c_{\mathrm{gev}},\, \lvert d_{\mathrm{gev}} - d \rvert,\, d]\bigr)\Bigr),\\
d_{\mathrm{fused}} &= \mathrm{softplus}\bigl(d + w\,(d_{\mathrm{gev}} - d)\bigr),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:failsoft}
\end{equation}
where $F$ is the fusion network whose final-layer bias is initialized to
$-4$, $c$ and $c_{\mathrm{gev}}$ the tile and geometry confidences, and
$w$ the blend weight; the slopes are attenuated by $(1 - w)$ and the
confidence becomes the maximum of the two estimates.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_3_6_gev_fusion_preview}
\caption{\textbf{Narrow-band geometry encoding volume and fail-soft fusion.} The volume samples 33 hypotheses in a $\pm 16$ band around the current tile disparity; a learned gate with bias initialized to $-4$ blends the GEV proposal into the tile estimate only where it helps.}
\label{fig:gev_fusion}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Plane Rendering and Learned Convex Upsampling}
The final one-quarter disparity is upsampled to full resolution in two
learned two-times stages. The first stage predicts masks from the
one-quarter left features and the second from the one-half left
features. For every output subpixel, a mask predicts nine weights over
the three-by-three coarse neighborhood, and a softmax makes the weights
non-negative and sum to one, so the upsampled disparity is a convex
combination of nearby coarse values. This is the same convex upsampling
used in RAFT-Stereo \cite{lipson2021raftstereo}. Before upsampling, a
plane-rendering step reconstructs each tile from its disparity and
slopes and applies an edge gate; this was the decisive fix in the
boundary-sharpness study of Chapter 8, where it reduced the bad-0.5 rate
by roughly 22 percent relative to the control. Convex upsampling can
select different source neighbors near a boundary, but it cannot invent
a disparity hypothesis absent from the one-quarter prediction, so thin
and distant structures remain a limitation.

\centering

\caption{\textbf{Learned convex upsampling.} Every output subpixel is a softmax-weighted convex combination of its $3\times 3$ coarse neighborhood, with masks predicted from the left features; two 2$\times$ stages recover full resolution.}

\subsection{From Disparity to 3D Reconstruction}
\label{sec:reconstruction_method}
The disparity map itself is an intermediate result; the goal of the
pipeline is the three-dimensional structure of the scene that the
disparity encodes. Once the disparity is known, every valid pixel
back-projects to a metric three-dimensional point through the pinhole
relations
\begin{equation}
Z = \frac{f\,B}{d}, \qquad
X = \frac{(u - c_x)\,Z}{f}, \qquad
Y = \frac{(v - c_y)\,Z}{f}
\label{eq:backprojection}
\end{equation}
where $(u, v)$ are the pixel coordinates, $(c_x, c_y)$ the principal
point, $f$ the focal length in pixels, $B$ the stereo baseline, and
$(X, Y, Z)$ the recovered point in the camera frame. Each point is
colored with the corresponding left-image pixel, which yields a dense
colored point cloud of the scene. For the reconstructions reported in
Chapter 8, the cloud is post-processed with two standard operations: a
statistical outlier filter removes the isolated flying points that
appear along depth discontinuities, and a voxel downsampling makes the
point density uniform. The stereo rig's calibrated values, a focal
length of approximately 1005 pixels at the native 1280-pixel width and
a baseline of 52 millimeters, scale to the inference resolution by the
width ratio. This reconstruction path is what the thesis title refers
to: the network provides the dense disparity, and the calibrated
geometry turns it into a metric three-dimensional model of the scene.

\section{Design Assessment and Performance Indices}
\label{sec:design_assessment}
Accuracy is reported with the standard stereo metrics in
\eqref{eq:metrics}: end-point error is the mean absolute disparity
error over valid pixels, bad-$t$ is the percentage of valid
pixels whose absolute error exceeds $t$ pixels, and D1-all is the
KITTI outlier definition. Root-mean-square error and median
absolute error are also reported: root-mean-square error is
sensitive to large outliers, while median absolute error gives an
outlier-robust summary. Each image contributes its own averaged
metric, so scores are averaged per image rather than pooled across
every pixel. Latency is measured at batch one, 384 by 640
resolution, with ten warm-up and a hundred timed passes; memory is
the peak inference working set, and the device used is stated
throughout.

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{EPE} &= \frac{1}{\lvert V \rvert} \sum_{p \in V}
  \bigl\lvert \hat{d}(p) - d^{*}(p) \bigr\rvert, \qquad
\mathrm{bad}\text{-}t = \frac{100}{\lvert V \rvert}\,
  \Bigl\lvert \bigl\{ p \in V :
  \lvert \hat{d}(p) - d^{*}(p) \rvert > t \bigr\} \Bigr\rvert,\\
\mathrm{D1} &= \frac{100}{\lvert V \rvert}\, \Bigl\lvert \bigl\{ p \in V :
  \lvert \hat{d}(p) - d^{*}(p) \rvert > 3 \;\wedge\;
  \lvert \hat{d}(p) - d^{*}(p) \rvert > 0.05\, d^{*}(p) \bigr\} \Bigr\rvert
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:metrics}
\end{equation}
where $V$ is the set of valid pixels, $\hat{d}$ the predicted disparity,
$d^{*}$ the ground truth, and $t \in \{0.5, 1, 2, 3\}$ pixels the bad-rate
thresholds.

Alongside accuracy, latency, and memory, the parameter budget is itself
a performance index, bounded by the envelope in Section
\ref{sec:design_requirements}. Figure \ref{fig:param_budget} reports the module-wise split
of the trained model; the split is computed directly from the
checkpoint's state dictionary.

\section{Summary}
This chapter defined the design problem, the four research questions,
and the hard envelope: under 3 million parameters, real-time
operation on a 4 to 6 TOPS accelerator, under 200 megabytes of
inference memory, and INT8-exportable operators. Candidate designs were
judged on the full eight-metric stereo set plus latency and
memory, under a matched-overfit protocol with mandatory cross-dataset
evaluation. The resulting architecture, StereoLite, combines a
group-wise cost volume, recurrent tile refinement with plane-aware
cross-scale propagation, a narrow-band geometry encoding volume with a
fail-soft gate, and plane rendering with learned convex upsampling.
Each block is specified with its governing equations, and the design
decisions are auditable through the evidence table. The chapter
closed with the performance indices used throughout the thesis;
training procedure and edge-inference optimization appear in Chapter
7, and experimental evaluation appears in Chapter 8.
