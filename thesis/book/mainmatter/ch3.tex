\chapter{Design of StereoLite}

\section{Introduction}
This chapter presents the design of StereoLite, the compact stereo
matching network developed in this thesis. It first states the problem
and the research questions, then fixes the hardware envelope the design
must satisfy and the criteria by which candidate designs are judged.
The controlled exploration methodology that produced the final
architecture is described next, followed by the system model, the
conceptual design and its literature ancestry, the record of
preliminary design decisions, and a block-by-block detailed design with
all governing equations. The chapter closes with the performance
indices used to assess the design. Training procedure and inference
optimization are treated in Chapter 7, and all experimental results in
Chapter 8.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Problem Statements}
Stereo matching takes a pair of rectified images and produces a dense
disparity map, from which metric depth follows directly through the
calibrated relation of \eqref{eq:depth_from_disparity}. This thesis
addresses that task under a hardware budget rather than an accuracy
budget alone; the requirements of edge-deployable stereo matching that
motivate this budget are reviewed in Chapter 2, and the resulting hard
envelope is stated in the next section. These requirements frame four
research questions. The first asks whether a network within a few
million parameters can reach competitive in-domain accuracy on
synthetic data. The second asks whether it generalizes zero-shot to an
unseen real domain. The third asks whether it meets the real-time
latency target on the intended device class. The fourth asks whether it
tolerates the small vertical misalignments that arise when
rectification is imperfect, which is the second objective of this
thesis. We assume rectified binocular input and a maximum disparity of
192 pixels at the inference resolution, and we train on synthetic data
with dense ground truth.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Design Requirements and Requisites}
\label{sec:design_requirements}
The design requirements follow from the class of edge accelerators
found on mobile robots and drones. They form a hard envelope, and a
candidate design that violates any one of them is rejected regardless
of its accuracy:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Under 3 million parameters.} The network must be
    compact enough for on-device memory and storage; parameter count is
    also the first-order proxy for the download and update footprint of
    a deployed model.
  \item \textbf{Real time on a 4 to 6 TOPS accelerator.} The intended
    device class delivers roughly four to six tera-operations per
    second, so the network must complete a forward pass within the
    frame budget of a live camera stream on such a device.
  \item \textbf{Under 200 megabytes of inference working set.} Peak
    activation memory during inference must stay small enough to
    coexist with the rest of a robot's software stack on a
    shared-memory edge board.
  \item \textbf{INT8-exportable operators.} Every operator on the
    inference path must quantize cleanly to eight-bit integer
    arithmetic, since the accelerators in this class reach their rated
    throughput only under integer execution.
\end{itemize}

\FloatBarrier
\section{Design Criteria}
Within the envelope of the previous section, candidate designs are
judged on balance rather than on any single number. Every design
decision is evaluated on the full eight-metric stereo set, comprising
the end-point error, the root-mean-square error, the median absolute
error, the bad-0.5, bad-1, bad-2, and bad-3 rates, and the D1-all rate,
together with inference latency and peak memory; these quantities are
defined formally in Section \ref{sec:design_assessment}. A variant that
improves one metric while degrading the others, or that buys accuracy
with latency the envelope cannot afford, is not accepted. Because
in-domain gains can mask a loss of generalization, a cross-dataset
zero-shot evaluation is mandatory after every training run. Finally,
all comparisons are made under equal conditions: the same training
pairs, the same random seed, and the same step budget, so that a
measured difference is attributable to the design change alone.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Design Steps}
The architectural choices in this chapter were made under a controlled
matched-overfit methodology rather than by intuition. Each candidate is
trained on a fixed set of SceneFlow pairs with a fixed seed, deterministic
convolution kernels, an identical step budget, and an identical evaluation
protocol, and all eight stereo metrics together with an inference-latency
benchmark are reported for every arm. Single knobs are varied one at a
time, the winning criterion is registered before a run, and every
candidate is checked against a held-out cross-dataset evaluation, so that
an in-domain gain that fails to transfer is not mistaken for progress. The
results of these studies appear in Chapter 8; this section states the
protocol that produced them.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Modeling of Proposed System}
Figure \ref{fig:architecture} gives the complete pipeline. The proposed
model, StereoLite, is a coarse-to-fine tile-hypothesis network with light
recurrent refinement. A shared feature encoder processes both views and a
separate left-image context encoder feeds the recurrent state; a
group-wise cost volume at the coarsest scale initializes a slanted tile
state; convolutional GRU updates refine the state across three scales
with plane-aware propagation between them; a narrow-band geometry
encoding volume corrects the estimate through a learned fail-soft gate;
and plane rendering with learned convex upsampling recovers the
full-resolution disparity.

\section{Modeling of Conceptual Design}
Conceptually, the pipeline is an assembly of five stages, each of which
adapts a mechanism from the literature reviewed in Chapter 2 to the edge
budget. The matching
cost is a group-wise correlation volume in the manner of GwcNet
\cite{guo2019gwcnet}, restricted to a single coarse scale. The refinement
operator is a convolutional gated recurrent unit and the final upsampling
is a learned convex combination, both adapted from RAFT-Stereo
\cite{lipson2021raftstereo} but run for only a handful of iterations at
coarse resolution. A quarter-resolution geometry encoding volume, in the
spirit of IGEV-Stereo \cite{xu2023igev}, supplies a global disparity
proposal that a learned gate blends into the recurrent estimate. Slanted
tile planes, following the HITNet paradigm \cite{tankovich2021hitnet},
carry a local surface hypothesis across scales so that disparity remains
sub-pixel without rebuilding a cost volume at every level. A shared
feature encoder feeds the matching path, and a separate left-image
context encoder feeds the recurrent state. The detailed design of each
stage follows in Section \ref{sec:detailed_design}.

\section{Detailed Design}
\label{sec:detailed_design}
This section specifies each block of the pipeline in the order data
flows through it, with the governing equations.

\subsection{Shared Feature Encoder and Context Encoder}
The matching network is a truncated YOLO26s backbone whose first seven
blocks are retained, producing features at one quarter, one eighth, and
one sixteenth resolution with 128, 256, and 256 channels. A detection
backbone is a deliberate choice rather than a convenience: its features
are pretrained to localize objects across scales, which is close to what
stereo matching needs from an encoder, and the encoder ablation of
Chapter 8 confirmed that this backbone beats both a hand-built GhostConv
stack and its own smaller sibling on every accuracy metric at a
two-millisecond cost. The detection neck and head above the one-sixteenth
stage are removed at construction rather than merely unused, because
dead blocks would waste parameters and forward compute. The two views
are concatenated along the batch dimension and pass through a single
shared invocation, which guarantees identical weights for both images.
A separate context encoder
processes the left image alone. It applies a strided convolution followed
by two GhostConv blocks with squeeze-and-excitation, and projects to a
32-channel quarter-resolution context map that is pooled to the coarser
scales. Matching evidence and recurrent context are kept in separate
streams for a specific reason. When the matching features also serve as
context, the recurrent unit cannot separate appearance from
correspondence; the RAFT-Stereo lineage \cite{lipson2021raftstereo}
established that a dedicated context path improves refinement, and the
same separation is adopted here.

\subsection{Tile Initialization and Cost Volume}
To seed the coarse-to-fine process, the tile initialization module builds
a group-wise correlation volume at one sixteenth resolution. For each of
eight feature groups and each integer disparity, the matching score is the
mean channel-wise product between the left features and the horizontally
shifted right features, given by \eqref{eq:gwc}. A small three-dimensional
convolutional network with an eight, sixteen, sixteen, one channel
schedule regularizes the volume, and a softmax along the disparity axis
yields a probability distribution. The initial disparity and confidence
follow from the soft-argmax and the distribution peak in
\eqref{eq:softargmax_init}. The plane slopes are initialized to zero and
the hidden feature is replaced by the coarsest context map. The 24
hypotheses at this scale span roughly 0 to 368 full-resolution pixels.

\begin{equation}
C(g,d,y,x) \;=\; \frac{1}{C/G}\sum_{c=1}^{C/G}
  f_{L}^{\,g,c}(y,x)\; f_{R}^{\,g,c}(y,\,x-d)
\label{eq:gwc}
\end{equation}
where $G = 8$ is the number of feature groups, $C$ the channel count,
$d \in \{0, \dots, 23\}$ the integer disparity hypotheses at one sixteenth
resolution, and $f_{L}^{\,g,c}$, $f_{R}^{\,g,c}$ the $c$-th channel of
group $g$ of the left and right feature maps.

\begin{equation}
d_{0} \;=\; \sum_{d} p(d)\, d, \qquad
c_{0} \;=\; \max_{d}\, p(d)
\label{eq:softargmax_init}
\end{equation}
where $p(d)$ is the softmax of the regularized cost volume along the
disparity dimension, $d_0$ the expected disparity, and $c_0$ the peak
probability that serves as an initial confidence.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{fig_3_3_tile_init_preview}
\caption{\textbf{Tile-state initialization at 1/16 resolution.} A group-wise correlation volume (8 groups, 24 hypotheses) is regularized by a small 3-D CNN; soft-argmax and the distribution maximum yield the initial disparity and confidence, and the context map replaces the zero hidden state.}
\label{fig:tile_init}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Recurrent Tile Refinement}
The tile state is refined by separate refinement modules at one
sixteenth, one eighth, and one quarter resolution; their weights are not
shared because the matching-feature channel counts differ across scales.
At each iteration the right feature map is warped toward the left view by
the current disparity, and a differentiable local correlation is
evaluated over five offsets within a half-range of two pixels. The
recurrent input assembles the left features, the warped right features,
the current state, the local correlation, and the context, as in
\eqref{eq:gru_input}. A convolutional gated recurrent unit updates the
persistent hidden state through the gate equations of \eqref{eq:convgru}.
A two-layer head with 48 hidden channels predicts residuals for
disparity, both plane slopes, and confidence, and a second learned gate
emits four independent sigmoid weights that scale each residual.
Disparity is constrained to remain non-negative through a softplus. The
schedule runs two iterations at one sixteenth, three at one eighth, and
three at one quarter, for eight learned updates in total.

\begin{equation}
x \;=\; \bigl[\, f_{L},\; \mathcal{W}(f_{R};\,d),\; d,\; s_{x},\; s_{y},\;
  c,\; \mathrm{corr},\; \mathrm{ctx} \,\bigr]
\label{eq:gru_input}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{W}(f_{R}; d)$ denotes the right features warped by the
current disparity, $\mathrm{corr}$ the five-offset local correlation, and
$\mathrm{ctx}$ the scale-matched context map.

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
z &= \sigma\!\bigl(\mathrm{Conv}([h,\,x])\bigr), &
r &= \sigma\!\bigl(\mathrm{Conv}([h,\,x])\bigr),\\
q &= \tanh\!\bigl(\mathrm{Conv}([r \odot h,\,x])\bigr), &
h' &= (1-z) \odot h + z \odot q,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:convgru}
\end{equation}
where $h$ is the 32-channel persistent hidden state, $z$ and $r$ the
update and reset gates, $q$ the candidate state, and $\odot$ the
element-wise product.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_3_4_refinement_preview}
\caption{\textbf{One recurrent refinement iteration.} The right features are warped by the current disparity, a five-offset local correlation is evaluated, and the assembled input updates the persistent hidden state through a convolutional GRU; four sigmoid gates control the residual updates of disparity, both slopes, and confidence.}
\label{fig:refinement}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Plane-Aware Cross-Scale Propagation}
Between adjacent scales, the propagation module resamples the disparity,
slopes, hidden feature, and confidence to the finer grid. Disparity is
multiplied by two because its pixel unit changes with resolution. Unlike
plain bilinear interpolation, the propagated disparity also uses the local
plane slopes to place the four child pixels around each parent tile,
following \eqref{eq:plane_prop}. This is the tile-plane component of the
design, in the manner of HITNet \cite{tankovich2021hitnet}; it preserves
a local slanted-surface hypothesis while moving to a finer grid, which
keeps sub-pixel structure without rebuilding a cost volume at every level.

\begin{equation}
d_{\mathrm{child}} \;=\; 2\,\mathcal{B}(d_{\mathrm{parent}})
  \;+\; 2\,s_{x}\,\Delta x \;+\; 2\,s_{y}\,\Delta y,
\qquad \Delta x, \Delta y \in \{-0.25,\, +0.25\}
\label{eq:plane_prop}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{B}$ is bilinear resampling of the parent disparity, $s_x$
and $s_y$ the local plane slopes, and $\Delta x, \Delta y \in \{-0.25,
+0.25\}$ the child offsets; the factor of two rescales the disparity to
the finer grid.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_3_5_plane_prop_preview}
\caption{\textbf{Plane-aware propagation between scales.} Each parent tile spawns four children whose disparities follow the local plane defined by the slopes, per \eqref{eq:plane_prop}, instead of plain bilinear interpolation.}
\label{fig:plane_prop}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Geometry Encoding Volume and Fail-Soft Fusion}
The geometry encoding volume adds an independent global proposal at one
quarter resolution, in the spirit of IGEV-Stereo \cite{xu2023igev}. In the
trained configuration it is narrow: rather than the full 64 disparity
planes of the base design, it samples 33 hypotheses in a band of plus or
minus 16 around the current tile disparity, a change validated in
Chapter 8 as accuracy-neutral while removing roughly a quarter of the
model's multiply-accumulate operations. The band is not as restrictive
as it sounds: 16 quarter-resolution planes correspond to 64
full-resolution pixels on either side of the current estimate, and since
the coarse initialization has already localized the disparity, the
band's job is correction rather than search. The full-range volume
spends most of its computation scoring hypotheses the tile state has
already ruled out, which is why removing them costs nothing. The volume is regularized by
three three-dimensional convolutions with 16 hidden channels, and the
softmax distribution yields the expected disparity, a confidence, and a
projected geometry feature, as in \eqref{eq:gev_expect}. The proposal is
not substituted unconditionally. A learned gate takes the context, the
geometry feature, both confidences, the absolute disagreement, and the
current disparity, and produces a scalar weight through
\eqref{eq:failsoft}. The gate's final bias is initialized to minus four,
so the initial weight is approximately 0.018 and training begins close to
the stable recurrent baseline; the network then learns where the geometry
proposal helps. This fail-soft design keeps the global branch from
corrupting regions the recurrent path already resolves well.

\begin{equation}
d_{\mathrm{gev}} = \sum_{d} p(d)\, d, \qquad
c_{\mathrm{gev}} = \max_{d}\, p(d), \qquad
g_{\mathrm{gev}} = \phi\Bigl(\sum_{d} G(d)\, p(d)\Bigr)
\label{eq:gev_expect}
\end{equation}
where $p(d)$ is the softmax over the regularized quarter-resolution
volume, $G(d)$ the regularized three-dimensional geometry feature, and
$\phi$ a projection to a 16-channel two-dimensional expected-geometry map.

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
w &= \sigma\!\Bigl(F\bigl([\mathrm{ctx}_{4},\, g_{\mathrm{gev}},\, c,\,
  c_{\mathrm{gev}},\, \lvert d_{\mathrm{gev}} - d \rvert,\, d]\bigr)\Bigr),\\
d_{\mathrm{fused}} &= \mathrm{softplus}\bigl(d + w\,(d_{\mathrm{gev}} - d)\bigr),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:failsoft}
\end{equation}
where $F$ is the fusion network whose final-layer bias is initialized to
$-4$, $c$ and $c_{\mathrm{gev}}$ the tile and geometry confidences, and
$w$ the blend weight; the slopes are attenuated by $(1 - w)$ and the
confidence becomes the maximum of the two estimates.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_3_6_gev_fusion_preview}
\caption{\textbf{Narrow-band geometry encoding volume and fail-soft fusion.} The volume samples 33 hypotheses in a $\pm 16$ band around the current tile disparity; a learned gate with bias initialized to $-4$ blends the GEV proposal into the tile estimate only where it helps.}
\label{fig:gev_fusion}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Plane Rendering and Learned Convex Upsampling}
The final one-quarter disparity is upsampled to full resolution in two
learned two-times stages. The first stage predicts masks from the
one-quarter left features and the second from the one-half left features.
For every output subpixel, a mask predicts nine weights over the
three-by-three coarse neighborhood, and a softmax makes the weights
non-negative and sum to one, so the upsampled disparity is a convex
combination of nearby coarse values, following the convex upsampling of
RAFT-Stereo \cite{lipson2021raftstereo}. Before upsampling, a
plane-rendering step reconstructs each tile from its disparity and slopes
and applies an edge gate; this was the decisive fix in the
boundary-sharpness study of Chapter 8, where it reduced the bad-0.5 rate
by roughly 22 percent relative to the control. Convex upsampling can
select different source neighbors near a boundary, but it cannot invent a
disparity hypothesis absent from the one-quarter prediction, so thin and
distant structures remain a known limitation.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{fig_3_7_upsample_preview}
\caption{\textbf{Learned convex upsampling.} Every output subpixel is a softmax-weighted convex combination of its $3\times 3$ coarse neighborhood, with masks predicted from the left features; two 2$\times$ stages recover full resolution.}
\label{fig:upsample}
\end{figure}

\subsection{From Disparity to 3D Reconstruction}
\label{sec:reconstruction_method}
The disparity map is not the end product of the pipeline; it is the
carrier of the three-dimensional structure of the scene. Once the
disparity is known, every valid pixel back-projects to a metric
three-dimensional point through the pinhole relations
\begin{equation}
Z = \frac{f\,B}{d}, \qquad
X = \frac{(u - c_x)\,Z}{f}, \qquad
Y = \frac{(v - c_y)\,Z}{f}
\label{eq:backprojection}
\end{equation}
where $(u, v)$ are the pixel coordinates, $(c_x, c_y)$ the principal
point, $f$ the focal length in pixels, $B$ the stereo baseline, and
$(X, Y, Z)$ the recovered point in the camera frame. Coloring each point
with the corresponding left-image pixel yields a dense colored point
cloud of the scene. For the reconstructions reported in Chapter 8, the
cloud is post-processed with two standard operations: a statistical
outlier filter removes the isolated flying points that appear along depth
discontinuities, and a voxel downsampling makes the point density
uniform. The stereo rig's calibrated values, a focal length of
approximately 1005 pixels at the native 1280-pixel width and a baseline
of 52 millimeters, scale to the inference resolution by the width ratio.
This reconstruction path is what the thesis title refers to: the network
provides the dense disparity, and the calibrated geometry turns it into a
metric three-dimensional model of the scene.

\section{Design Assessment and Performance Indices}
\label{sec:design_assessment}
Accuracy is reported with the standard stereo metrics defined in
\eqref{eq:metrics}. The end-point error is the mean absolute disparity
error over valid pixels; the bad-$t$ rate is the percentage of valid
pixels whose absolute error exceeds $t$ pixels; and the D1-all rate is the
KITTI outlier definition. The root-mean-square error and the median
absolute error are reported alongside, the former to expose large outliers
and the latter as an outlier-robust summary. Each image contributes its
own averaged metric, and the scores are averaged over images rather than
by pooling every pixel globally. Latency is measured at batch one and
384 by 640 resolution with ten warm-up passes and a hundred timed passes,
and memory as the peak inference working set; the device is stated for
every reported measurement.

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{EPE} &= \frac{1}{\lvert V \rvert} \sum_{p \in V}
  \bigl\lvert \hat{d}(p) - d^{*}(p) \bigr\rvert, \qquad
\mathrm{bad}\text{-}t = \frac{100}{\lvert V \rvert}\,
  \Bigl\lvert \bigl\{ p \in V :
  \lvert \hat{d}(p) - d^{*}(p) \rvert > t \bigr\} \Bigr\rvert,\\
\mathrm{D1} &= \frac{100}{\lvert V \rvert}\, \Bigl\lvert \bigl\{ p \in V :
  \lvert \hat{d}(p) - d^{*}(p) \rvert > 3 \;\wedge\;
  \lvert \hat{d}(p) - d^{*}(p) \rvert > 0.05\, d^{*}(p) \bigr\} \Bigr\rvert
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:metrics}
\end{equation}
where $V$ is the set of valid pixels, $\hat{d}$ the predicted disparity,
$d^{*}$ the ground truth, and $t \in \{0.5, 1, 2, 3\}$ pixels the bad-rate
thresholds.

Alongside accuracy, latency, and memory, the parameter budget itself is
a performance index of the design, since the envelope of Section
\ref{sec:design_requirements} bounds it. Figure \ref{fig:param_budget} reports the module-wise split
of the trained model, computed directly from the checkpoint's state
dictionary.

\section{Summary}
This chapter defined the design problem, the four research questions,
and the hard envelope of under 3 million parameters, real-time
operation on a 4 to 6 TOPS accelerator, under 200 megabytes of
inference memory, and INT8-exportable operators. Candidate designs were
judged on the full eight-metric stereo set together with latency and
memory, under a matched-overfit protocol with mandatory cross-dataset
evaluation. The resulting architecture, StereoLite, combines a
group-wise cost volume, recurrent tile refinement with plane-aware
cross-scale propagation, a narrow-band geometry encoding volume with a
fail-soft gate, and plane rendering with learned convex upsampling,
each block specified with its governing equations. The design decisions
