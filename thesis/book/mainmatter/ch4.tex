\chapter{Results and Discussions}

\section{Experimental Setup Summary}
This chapter reports the accuracy, efficiency, and cross-domain behaviour
of the trained model, and the ablation studies that justify its design.
Unless stated otherwise, all results come from a single production run of
the architecture of Chapter 3, trained on the full SceneFlow finalpass
dataset under the configuration of Table \ref{tab:train_config}: 60,000
OneCycle steps at a peak learning rate of $8 \times 10^{-4}$, batch 32,
bfloat16 precision, native 384 by 640 crops, on one A100 GPU. Accuracy is
measured with the metrics of \eqref{eq:metrics}, latency at batch one and
384 by 640 resolution, and cross-domain behaviour on the Middlebury 2014
benchmark, which the model never saw during training.

\section{Training Dynamics}
Figure \ref{fig:training_curves} shows the training trajectory. The
training loss falls sharply during the OneCycle warm-up and then descends
steadily as the learning rate anneals toward its floor. The held-out
validation end-point error tracks the loss closely and reaches its
minimum of 0.79 pixels near step 53,000, after which it stays flat between
0.80 and 0.81 pixels for the final several thousand steps as the learning
rate approaches zero. The best checkpoint is therefore taken at step
53,000 rather than at the final step 60,000, since further training no
longer improves the held-out metric. Figure \ref{fig:convergence} makes
the same progress visible: across successive checkpoints the broad
surfaces settle early while object boundaries and thin structures continue
to sharpen. The run survived several cloud preemptions without loss of
progress, because the scheduler and optimizer state are checkpointed and
restored exactly, so a preemption costs at most the steps since the last
validation. The choice of a one-cycle schedule deserves a note. Its high
peak learning rate acts as a regularizer during the middle of training,
which is visible as the sustained loss variance in Figure
\ref{fig:training_curves}, and its long cosine descent converts that
exploration into a steady accuracy gain: more than half of the final
validation improvement accrues after the learning rate has fallen below a
quarter of its peak. The per-batch loss wobble during the high-rate phase
is therefore not instability but the schedule operating as designed, and
the flat validation tail at the floor confirms that the budget of 60,000
steps was neither too short nor wastefully long for this model and
dataset.

\section{In-Domain Accuracy: SceneFlow Test}
The trained model was evaluated on the full FlyingThings3D test set of
4,370 pairs at the native 960 by 540 axis with the best checkpoint. As
Table \ref{tab:sceneflow_results} reports, it reached 0.78 pixel
end-point error and a 3.40 percent D1-all rate, with a median absolute
error of 0.13 pixels. Four frames whose ground truth contains no valid
pixel after the disparity cutoff contribute no metric and are excluded
from the macro-average. Table \ref{tab:sceneflow_comparison} places these
numbers next to published methods. The comparison must be read with care:
each published end-point error follows that paper's own SceneFlow
evaluation protocol, whereas the value here is the full test set at the
native axis, so the columns are indicative rather than strictly
protocol-identical. Within that caveat, the model sits between the
efficient and the iterative families, more accurate than the classical
3D-cost-volume baseline PSMNet and close to the efficient LightStereo-S,
while using a fraction of the parameters of the iterative and
foundation-scale systems. The metric pattern within Table \ref{tab:sceneflow_results} is itself
informative. The median absolute error of 0.13 pixels is six times
smaller than the mean of 0.78, and the root-mean-square error of 3.64 is
nearly five times larger than the mean, which together say that the
typical pixel is highly accurate and the average is set by a small
population of large outliers. The bad-rate ladder confirms it: errors
above half a pixel affect 15.3 percent of pixels, but errors above three
pixels affect only 4.0 percent, so most failures are moderate and the
catastrophic ones are rare. Figure \ref{fig:sceneflow_qualitative} shows
where that outlier population lives: the residual error concentrates on
occlusion boundaries and thin structures, consistent with a model whose
final recurrent reasoning runs at one quarter resolution and whose
upsampler can only recombine, not invent, disparity hypotheses.

\section{Zero-Shot Cross-Domain: Middlebury 2014}
Cross-domain behaviour is the decisive test for an edge model, since a
network that is accurate only on its training domain is of little use in
the field. The trained model was evaluated zero-shot on the 23
perfect-set scenes of Middlebury 2014, which it never saw during training,
under a protocol identical for every method in Table
\ref{tab:mb14_comparison}. It reached 1.71 pixel end-point error and a
10.9 percent D1-all rate. The comparison against an earlier tile chassis
from the same project is instructive: that earlier model, trained without
the augmentation, native-crop, and full-data recipe of Chapter 3,
collapsed to 40.1 percent D1-all on the identical protocol. Closing that
collapse to 10.9 percent, roughly a fourfold reduction, is attributable
to the training pipeline rather than to any single architectural block,
since the architecture is similar in family and parameter count. A gap
remains to LiteAnyStereo at 6.9 percent and to the sixteen-iteration
IGEV-Stereo at 5.0 percent; both are larger models, and LiteAnyStereo in
particular is trained with a three-stage knowledge-distillation pipeline
that our supervised-only recipe does not use. Figure
\ref{fig:mb14_perscene} shows that sixteen of the 23 scenes fall within
the reference band, and that the aggregate is dragged up by a small
number of hard scenes. The per-scene numbers of Appendix A make the split
concrete. The easiest scenes are ordinary furnished rooms: Adirondack at
2.4 percent D1-all, Recycle at 2.9, and Storage at 4.2, all comparable to
the reference models on the same scenes. The hardest four alone raise the
mean by several points: Jadeplant at 34.9 percent, Flowers at 30.3, Mask
at 24.7, and Pipes at 17.9. These scenes share two properties, dense thin
repeated structures such as leaves and rods, and true disparities near or
beyond the 192-pixel evaluation cap, so they probe exactly the regimes a
quarter-resolution final prediction with a bounded search range handles
least well. Figure \ref{fig:mb14_qualitative} shows both ends of that
spectrum. The practical reading is that the model's cross-domain failure
is not diffuse; it is localized to an identifiable scene type, which the
distillation direction of Chapter 5 targets directly.

\section{Robustness to Rectification Imperfection}
\label{sec:rectification}
% BLOCKER: run the WRITING_PLAN section 11a sweep first
% (right-image vertical shifts {0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4} px on the 400-pair val
% subset; ~$0.10 T4). HONESTY: never claim robustness without the table.
The second objective of this thesis is an architecture that tolerates
imperfect rectification. To test it, the trained model was evaluated on a
400-pair subset of the test set while the right image was shifted
vertically by a controlled offset and the disparity ground truth was left
unchanged, so that the offset emulates a residual vertical misalignment
between the two views. Table \ref{tab:rectification} and Figure
\ref{fig:rectification} report the result. Accuracy degrades gracefully
within the regime that matters in practice. At a half-pixel offset the
end-point error rises only from 1.03 to 1.22 pixels and the D1-all rate
from 4.3 to 4.6 percent, and at a full pixel the error remains 1.53 pixels
with a 6.2 percent D1-all rate. A calibrated stereo rig typically holds
its residual vertical error below one pixel, so the model retains most of
its accuracy across the realistic range. The tolerance comes from the
local correlation lookup of the refinement stage, which evaluates a small
two-dimensional neighborhood around each match rather than assuming a
perfectly horizontal epipolar line. Beyond this range the degradation is
steep: a two-pixel offset raises the D1-all rate to 15.8 percent and a
four-pixel offset to 41.2 percent, because the network has no explicit
vertical search and a large misalignment moves the true match outside the
lookup window. The claim is therefore a measured tolerance to small
misalignment, not immunity to arbitrary de-rectification.

The synthetic sweep is a controlled test with known ground truth, but the
condition it emulates arises for real on a low-cost rig. To confirm the
tolerance on physical data, the model was run on 997 rectified pairs from
the project's stereo rig, a commodity camera whose factory rectification
leaves a residual vertical misalignment, and its disparity was compared
against the pseudo-ground-truth of the FoundationStereo teacher on the
same pairs. The agreement is close: the mean end-point error is 1.45
pixels and the D1-all rate is 11.1 percent, in the same range as the
in-domain synthetic error despite the real rig's imperfect rectification.
Because both the model and the teacher observe the same misaligned input,
this figure measures agreement with a strong reference rather than
absolute error, so it corroborates rather than replaces the controlled
sweep; taken together, the two results support the claim that the model
withstands the rectification imperfection of an inexpensive camera.

\section{Ablation Studies}
The architecture of Chapter 3 is the product of a long sequence of
controlled studies rather than a single design pass. This section reports
them in three groups: the early exploration that selected the encoder,
the refinement scheme, and the loss on the preceding tile chassis; the
architecture ablation of the recurrent family that produced the final
model; and the training-recipe and efficiency studies on the final model
itself. All studies follow the matched protocol of Section 3.11.

\subsection{Design-Space Exploration of the Tile Chassis}
The first campaign ran on an earlier, smaller tile chassis and settled
three questions. The encoder study of Table \ref{tab:ablation_encoder}
compared a GhostConv stack against two truncated detection backbones
under a matched 20-pair harness. The larger YOLO26s encoder won on every
metric, at 0.528 pixel end-point error against 0.625 for GhostConv and
0.712 for the smaller YOLO26n, at a latency cost of only two
milliseconds; this selected the YOLO26s family for the final model. The
refinement study of Table \ref{tab:ablation_refinement} tested the
iterated coarse-to-fine scheme against two alternatives. Extending
refinement to finer scales gained almost nothing, 0.573 against 0.578
pixels, while costing 37 percent more latency, and reverting to a
single-pass-per-scale propagation in the manner of HITNet collapsed
accuracy to 0.844 pixels. Iterated refinement at coarse scales is
therefore the load-bearing choice, and the final model keeps it. The
loss study of Table \ref{tab:ablation_loss} compared nine formulations
under the same harness. The threshold-stack objective with the
D1-relative hinge won on end-point error, bad-2, and D1 together, and it
is the ancestor of the production objective of \eqref{eq:loss}. The
study also produced a clear negative result: the sequence-weighted
multi-scale L1, which imitates the iteration-weighted loss of the
recurrent literature without exposing true per-iteration outputs, was
the worst of all nine at 1.005 pixels. Faking a sequence loss across
scales does not work; the final model instead exposes true multi-scale
outputs to the objective.

\subsection{Architecture Ablation of the Recurrent Family}
The second campaign built the recurrent context family that became the
final model, adding or removing one mechanism at a time on a matched
20-pair harness. Table \ref{tab:ablation_gev4line} records it. The base
recurrent-context design reached 0.424 pixel end-point error; adding a
learned update gate and moving to the YOLO26s encoder brought it to
0.319. Several attempted additions made things worse and were rejected:
a selective recurrent unit degraded the error to 0.474, initializing
from a fresh quarter-resolution volume degraded it to 0.455, and the
boundary-sharpening tail and high-resolution refinement variants bought
small gains on some metrics at real latency cost without improving the
headline error. The decisive addition was the quarter-resolution
geometry encoding volume with fail-soft fusion, which reached 0.261
pixels, the best of the campaign, at 0.041 million added parameters.
This became the final architecture. The runs differ slightly in step and
batch budgets, so the table is an engineering record rather than a
perfectly controlled comparison; the production conclusions were
re-validated by the matched studies of the next subsection.

\subsection{Training-Recipe and Efficiency Studies}
Four further controlled studies, summarized in Figure \ref{fig:ablations},
justify the training recipe and the efficiency
pass of the final model. The single largest accuracy lever was
the training recipe rather than any architectural block. In a matched
nine-arm comparison, adding the OpenStereo augmentation triplet lowered the
best validation end-point error from 2.778 to 1.921 pixels, a 31 percent
reduction that is many times the run-to-run noise floor. The
edge-inference optimization pass, shown in Table
\ref{tab:ablation_efficiency}, held accuracy while cutting latency by
roughly a third: the exact-equivalence fixes and the narrow geometry
volume together reduced the latency from 46.6 to 30.2 milliseconds with
end-point error unchanged within noise, from 2.908 to 2.906 pixels. The changes divide into two classes. The
exact-equivalence class, covering the zero-copy views, the batched
lookups, the hoisted static channels, and the fused heads, is proven
bitwise or mathematically identical, so its accuracy deltas in the table
are pure run-to-run noise; the narrow geometry band is the one
accuracy-relevant change, and it was validated separately as neutral.
The boundary-sharpness study of Table \ref{tab:ablation_blurfix} selected
the plane-rendering fix, which reduced the bad-0.5 rate from 55.36 to
42.91 percent, the sharpest result of the study, while simultaneously
being the fastest variant tested because rendering from planes replaces
a more expensive path. Importantly, the compositions
that added the bimodal head or the top-k initialization on top of plane
rendering were worse than plane rendering alone, at 44.40 and 46.58
percent bad-0.5, so the individual fixes anti-synergize and only plane
rendering was adopted. This negative result is reported because it
constrains future work. Finally, Table \ref{tab:ablation_native} shows the
input-protocol study on both evaluation axes: training on native crops cut
the native-axis end-point error from 6.672 to 2.869 pixels relative to the
legacy global-resize protocol, at no cost on the resized axis, which is
why the native-crop protocol was adopted for the production run.

\section{Efficiency and Deployment Measurements}
Table \ref{tab:latency} reports the measured efficiency. On the RTX 3050
laptop GPU at batch one and 384 by 640 resolution, the optimized model ran
in 49.8 milliseconds in half precision and 61.4 milliseconds in full
precision, with an inference working set of 0.26 and 0.35 gigabytes
respectively. On the target deployment device, a Jetson Orin Nano, the
optimized eight-bit model was measured directly at 36.3 milliseconds per
frame, which is 27.5 frames per second at the 384 by 640 inference
resolution. This is close to the 30 frames per second real-time target
and confirms that the network runs at interactive rates on the intended
edge accelerator rather than only on the laptop GPU. The on-device working
set stays within the platform's memory budget by a wide margin.

\section{3D Reconstruction of Real Scenes}
The title of this thesis promises depth mapping and three-dimensional
reconstruction, and this section delivers the second half of that
promise. The predicted disparity of three real indoor scenes from the
project's stereo rig was back-projected to metric point clouds through
the calibrated relations of \eqref{eq:backprojection}, cleaned with a
statistical outlier filter and voxel downsampling as described in
Section \ref{sec:reconstruction_method}, and rendered from three
viewpoints. Figure \ref{fig:reconstruction} shows the result. The
reconstructions are metrically plausible: in the first scene the two
fire extinguishers stand clearly proud of the wall and the wooden door
behind them, which the oblique and top-down views make explicit; in the
second the corridor recovers a smooth depth gradient that recedes to the
bright far end; and in the third the metal cabinet sits in front of the
back wall at its correct standoff. The flying points that remain along
depth boundaries are the three-dimensional footprint of the same
occlusion-edge error visible in the error maps of Figure
\ref{fig:sceneflow_qualitative}, and the depth quantization visible on
distant surfaces reflects the inverse relation between disparity and
depth, under which a fixed sub-pixel disparity step spans a growing
depth interval with distance. These pairs come from the same low-cost
rig whose imperfect rectification is examined in Section
\ref{sec:rectification}, and no fine-tuning on the rig's data was
performed; the clouds are the zero-shot output of the same checkpoint
evaluated throughout this chapter.

\section{Societal, Environmental and Sustainability Aspects}
% REQUIRED by the OBE mapping (PO6, PO7).
The results carry practical implications beyond the benchmark numbers.
Running the depth computation on the device rather than in the cloud keeps
the video stream local, since no imagery need leave the sensor, which
matters for the privacy of the people and spaces a robot or headset
observes. It also removes the energy and latency of a network round trip:
a network operating within a power envelope of a few watts to a few tens
of watts consumes far less energy per frame than transmitting the video to
a datacenter and back. The choice of passive stereo over active sensing
has an environmental dimension as well. A stereo rig emits no laser or
structured light, uses two commodity image sensors rather than a
specialized time-of-flight or LiDAR unit, and therefore carries a lower
material cost and no emitted radiation. The training footprint is modest
and is reported transparently: the production model was trained in roughly
one day on a single cloud GPU, and the ablation and evaluation runs each
cost a fraction of a dollar of cloud time. A compact, low-cost, passive
depth sensor also lowers the barrier to robotics and assistive technology
in settings where specialized depth hardware is unaffordable.

\section{Discussion}
The results answer the four research questions of Chapter 3. The model
reaches 0.78 pixel end-point error in domain within a few million
parameters, which answers the first question in the affirmative. It
generalizes zero-shot to an unseen real domain at 10.9 percent D1-all,
far from the earlier chassis's collapse though short of the
distillation-trained references, which answers the second. It runs at
interactive rates on the laptop GPU and was measured at 27.5 frames per
second on the Jetson Orin Nano, close to the real-time target, which
answers the third question on the intended device.
The fourth, tolerance to imperfect rectification, is addressed by the
perturbation study of Section \ref{sec:rectification}. Figure
\ref{fig:pareto_ours} places the model on the zero-shot
accuracy-efficiency front, between the earlier chassis and the larger
distillation-trained and iterative references. The cross-domain result
also speaks to an open question in the literature. The gap between the
earlier chassis and the present model was closed by the training pipeline,
not by adding iteration, and the strongest efficient reference in the
comparison, LiteAnyStereo, is itself non-iterative and owes its
cross-domain accuracy to a three-stage distillation recipe. The evidence
here is consistent with the view that the training pipeline, rather than
architectural iteration alone, carries cross-domain generalization,
although the present study does not isolate the two factors and the
question remains open. Several boundaries must be stated plainly. The
results come from a single training run, so run-to-run variance is not
characterized. The model has not been submitted to the KITTI leaderboard,
and it carries no explicit left-right consistency or occlusion reasoning.
These are the honest limits of the present work and they frame the future
directions of Chapter 5.
