\chapter{Addressing Complex Engineering Problems and Activities}

\section{Introduction}
The accreditation framework of the Washington Accord, which the BAETE
program-outcome system adopts, distinguishes routine engineering work
from work that addresses a Complex Engineering Problem (CEP) and involves
Complex Engineering Activities (CEA). A problem is complex when it cannot
be resolved without in-depth engineering knowledge. This complexity is
captured by a set of P attributes, the first of which, depth of
knowledge, is mandatory. An activity is complex when carrying it out
demands the A attributes, such as diverse resources, conflicting
requirements, and innovation. This chapter demonstrates that the present thesis satisfies
both. Section 5.2 maps the P attributes of the problem to the concrete
parts of this thesis that exhibit them, and Section 5.3 does the same
for the A attributes of the activities performed.

\section{Addressing Complex Engineering Problems}
Designing the network of this thesis is a complex engineering problem in
the formal sense: it cannot be solved by natural science or mathematics
alone. It requires in-depth engineering knowledge of the stereo
pipeline, specialist knowledge of the deep-stereo literature and its
building blocks, the design of a novel architecture under hard resource
constraints, and grounding in a large body of research literature to
select and combine the right mechanisms. In Washington Accord
knowledge-profile terms, these four demands correspond to profiles K3,
K4, K5, and K8. Attribute K3, engineering fundamentals, is exercised in
the stereo pipeline and the cost-volume mechanisms of Chapters 2 and 3.
Attribute K4, specialist knowledge, is exercised in the selection and
adaptation of the tile-hypothesis, recurrent, and geometry-volume
building blocks in Chapter 3. Attribute K5, engineering design, is
exercised in the design of the StereoLite architecture in Chapter 3 and
its inference-optimization pass under hard resource constraints in
Chapter 7. Attribute K8, research-literature grounding, is exercised in
the literature review of Chapter 2, which grounds every design choice in
published evidence.

The attributes in the table are not asserted abstractly; each points to a
concrete artifact. The conflicting requirements of P2 are the hard design
envelope stated in Chapter 3 and verified by the efficiency measurements
of Chapter 8. The depth of analysis of P3 is the ablation record of
Chapter 8, in which candidate mechanisms were added one at a time under a
matched protocol and several were rejected because they degraded
accuracy. The interdependence of P7 is visible in the same record: the
value of one block depends on which others are present, which is why
the design proceeded by controlled composition rather than independent
selection.

\section{Addressing Complex Engineering Activities}
The Washington Accord likewise characterizes complex engineering
activities by five A attributes. The work carried out in this thesis
exhibits each of them.

\textbf{A1, range of resources.} The activity used several distinct
kinds of resources: rented cloud GPUs for training and ablation,
multiple public stereo datasets for training and cross-domain
evaluation, a physical calibrated stereo camera rig for real-data
experiments, an embedded accelerator as the deployment target, and the
open-source deep-learning tooling on which the implementation of
Chapter 7 is built. Chapter 6 documents the management of these
resources, including their cost.

\textbf{A2, level of interaction.} The project's central technical
interactions are the conflicts among accuracy, latency, and memory:
improving one routinely degrades another. These conflicts could not be
resolved analytically. They were resolved instead through the
controlled ablation methodology of Chapter 3, under which every
candidate change was measured on all metrics jointly and adopted only
when the trade-off was favorable. Chapter 8 records these studies.

\textbf{A3, innovation.} The activity was creative rather than routine.
The architecture of Chapter 3 is a novel composition of building blocks
adapted from distinct families of the literature. It also includes the
fail-soft fusion of the geometry encoding volume, a mechanism designed
in this work so that the added evidence sharpens the estimate when it
is reliable and degrades gracefully, rather than catastrophically, when
it is not.

\textbf{A4, consequences to society and the environment.} The activity
has identifiable consequences beyond the laboratory: on-device privacy,
robot and drone safety, eye-safe passive sensing, accessibility of
low-cost depth, energy saved by avoiding cloud round trips, and a
transparent training footprint. Chapter 4 examines these consequences.

\textbf{A5, familiarity.} The activity extends beyond textbook material
and prior experience. Its subject matter (compact recurrent stereo
architectures, cross-domain generalization, and edge-inference
optimization) exists only in the recent research literature reviewed
in Chapter 2. Engaging with it required reading, evaluating, and
adapting published research rather than applying established practice.

\section{Summary}
This chapter demonstrated that the thesis addresses a complex
engineering problem and that the work carried out constitutes complex
engineering activities in the Washington Accord sense. The problem
satisfies the mandatory attribute P1 through the knowledge profile K3,
K4, K5, and K8. Table \ref{tab:cep_attributes} grounds the remaining
P attributes in concrete parts of the thesis: the conflicting
requirements of the edge envelope, the ablation-driven depth of
analysis, and the interdependence of the architectural blocks. The
activities exhibit the five A attributes through the range of
resources used, the measured resolution of conflicting requirements, the
novel block composition with fail-soft fusion, the societal and
environmental consequences of Chapter 4, and the beyond-textbook
research grounding of Chapter 2.
