\chapter{Socio-Economic Impact and Sustainability}
\label{ch:socio}

\section{Introduction}
The results of this thesis carry practical implications beyond the
benchmark numbers, and this chapter states them. The system under
discussion is the compact stereo matching network designed in
\ref{ch:design}, implemented and deployed in \ref{ch:implementation},
and evaluated in \ref{ch:results}: a passive depth sensor built from
two commodity image sensors and an embedded accelerator, whose entire
depth computation runs on the device. Each property of that design
(on-device processing, passive sensing, and a small computational
footprint) translates into a concrete societal or environmental
consequence. Section~\ref{sec:7-2} examines the societal, health,
safety, legal, and cultural dimensions; Section~\ref{sec:7-3} examines
the environmental and sustainability dimensions; Section~\ref{sec:7-4}
summarizes.

\section{Impact of the Thesis on Societal, Health, Safety, Legal and Cultural Issues}
\label{sec:7-2}
The most direct societal consequence of this work follows from where the
computation happens. Because the network runs the depth computation on
the device rather than in the cloud, the video stream stays local: no
imagery of the people or spaces that a robot, drone, or headset observes
ever needs to leave the sensor. This matters for privacy in a way that a
cloud-processed depth pipeline cannot match, since a system that
transmits continuous video to a remote server creates, by construction, a
stream of personal imagery that must then be secured, retained, and
governed. Keeping the raw imagery on the device removes that stream at
the source. The same property eases the legal and cultural concerns that
accompany camera-carrying machines. Regulations on personal-data
transfer, and the reasonable discomfort of people who share a space with
an observing device, both attach primarily to imagery that is recorded
or transmitted; an embedded sensor that consumes its own video internally
and emits only geometry is easier to deploy responsibly in homes,
workplaces, and public settings.

Safety is the second dimension. A mobile robot or drone acts safely in
three-dimensional space only to the extent that its depth perception is
reliable, and the evaluation of \ref{ch:results} addressed exactly the failure
modes that make depth unreliable in the field: the model was tested not
only in its training domain but zero-shot on unseen real scenes, under
controlled rectification error, and on the physical stereo rig itself.
On-device inference also removes the network round trip from the control
loop, so the depth estimate a robot acts on does not depend on the
availability or latency of a wireless link; a platform whose obstacle
avoidance survives a lost connection is categorically safer than one
whose perception stalls with the network. The choice of sensing modality
also carries health implications. A passive stereo rig emits no laser and
no structured infrared light; it only observes. Bystanders are therefore
not exposed to any emitted radiation, a relevant distinction from active
depth sensors, whose emitters must be engineered and certified for eye
safety, particularly around children and in crowded indoor spaces.

Finally, the work has an accessibility dimension. A compact, low-cost,
passive depth sensor lowers the barrier to robotics and assistive
technology in settings where specialized depth hardware is unaffordable.
Depth perception built from two commodity image sensors and an embedded
accelerator puts spatial sensing within reach of student projects,
small workshops, assistive devices, and products aimed at price-sensitive
markets, communities that a sensor costing several times as much would
simply exclude. This broadening of who can build and afford spatially
aware machines is itself a societal outcome, and it follows directly
from the design constraints this thesis set out to satisfy.

\section{Impact of the Thesis on the Environment and Sustainability}
\label{sec:7-3}
The environmental case for on-device stereo begins with energy. The
deployed network operates within a power envelope of a few watts to a few
tens of watts, and computing the depth locally consumes far less energy
per frame than transmitting the video stream to a datacenter and back.
A cloud-processed alternative pays the energy of radio transmission, of
the network infrastructure along the path, and of the datacenter
inference itself, for every frame, over the entire operating life of the
device. On-device inference removes that recurring per-frame transport
cost, so the energy advantage grows over the deployed lifetime of every
unit.

The choice of passive stereo over active sensing also matters for
materials. A stereo rig uses two commodity image sensors rather than a
specialized time-of-flight or LiDAR unit, and it emits no laser or
structured light. It therefore carries no emitter hardware at all: no
laser diode, no pattern projector, and none of the associated drive
electronics. Commodity image sensors are manufactured at large scale for
the phone and webcam markets, so a depth sensor assembled from them draws
on the low material cost and established supply chain of that scale,
rather than requiring the specialized components of an active unit. Fewer
and more common parts also make the sensor easier to source, repair, and
replace over its service life, which are practical measures of hardware
sustainability.

Sustainability also applies to the development of the model itself, and
this thesis reports its training footprint. The production model was
trained in roughly one day on a single cloud GPU, and the ablation and
evaluation runs each cost a fraction of a dollar of cloud time. This
footprint is deliberately modest by the standards of modern deep
learning, where the largest stereo systems are built on foundation-scale
pretraining whose compute is orders of magnitude larger. The methodology
that made this possible, the small controlled ablation harness of
\ref{ch:design} that filtered design candidates cheaply before the single
full training run of \ref{ch:implementation}, is itself a sustainability
practice: compute was spent once, on a design already validated by that
cheaper process.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Summary}
\label{sec:7-4}
This chapter traced the design properties of the system to their
societal and environmental consequences. On-device inference keeps
imagery local, which protects the privacy of observed people and spaces
and eases legal and cultural concerns; reliable, connection-independent
depth makes robots and drones safer; passive sensing emits no laser and
poses no eye-safety hazard; and the low cost of the sensor widens access
to assistive and robotic technology. Environmentally, local computation
avoids the recurring energy cost of a cloud round trip, the passive rig
needs no specialized emitter hardware beyond two commodity image
sensors, and the model's training footprint is small: roughly one day
on a single cloud GPU, with ablations costing a fraction of a dollar
each. These impacts follow directly from the edge-deployment
constraints that shaped the design.
