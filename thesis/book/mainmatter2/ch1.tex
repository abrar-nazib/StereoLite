\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}

\section{Overview}
A machine that must act in three-dimensional space first needs to know
how far away things are. A mobile robot needs it to avoid obstacles, a
drone needs it to hold position, and an augmented-reality headset needs
it to anchor virtual content to real surfaces. Binocular stereo recovers
this depth passively from two inexpensive image sensors, but the dense
correspondence it requires is computationally demanding, and the deep
networks that now solve it most accurately are far too large and too slow
for the embedded accelerators these platforms carry. This thesis designs,
trains, and deploys a compact stereo matching network that narrows this
gap. The network produces dense disparity, and from it metric
three-dimensional reconstruction, within the parameter, latency, and
memory budget of edge hardware.

The problem it addresses is a joint one: dense and accurate stereo depth
on hardware that supplies only a few tera-operations per second, a few
gigabytes of memory, and a few watts of power. This chapter states the
background and motivation, the research questions the thesis answers, the
objectives, the key contributions, and the organization of the work.

\section{Background and Motivation}
Depth is not an optional cue. It is the quantity on which the safety and
usefulness of spatially acting systems depend, and the sensing options
that supply it differ sharply in cost and character. Scanning LiDAR
measures time of flight along emitted laser beams and returns accurate but
sparse points, at a price and power draw that exclude small platforms.
Structured-light and time-of-flight cameras emit infrared patterns or
pulses and recover dense depth indoors, but their emitters limit range,
fail in sunlight, and interfere with one another when several devices
share a space. Among the passive ways to recover depth, binocular stereo
suits embedded systems because it emits nothing, draws little power, works
indoors and outdoors alike, and uses two inexpensive image sensors rather
than a laser scanner. Its cost is computational. The correspondence
between the two views must be solved for every pixel, and that computation
is what this thesis sets out to make affordable.

Binocular stereo recovers depth from the horizontal shift of a scene
point between two horizontally separated views. That shift, the
disparity, is inversely proportional to depth through a calibrated
pinhole relation. A dense disparity map
therefore yields a dense depth map, which downstream perception and
reconstruction consume directly, and computing that disparity map
accurately and quickly is the task this thesis addresses.

The systems considered in this thesis operate under similar constraints:
limited computational resources, restricted power availability, and a
need for real-time estimates. Their depth requirements, however, are not
identical. A mobile robot primarily requires dependable obstacle depth
over short and medium ranges, whereas a drone also demands a high update
rate because it may approach obstacles more rapidly. In augmented
reality, accurate object boundaries are especially important for
realistic occlusion between virtual and physical content. A compact
sensing platform, by contrast, places greater emphasis on metric accuracy
for measurement and three-dimensional reconstruction. An embedded stereo
network intended for these settings must therefore achieve a practical
compromise among accuracy, boundary fidelity, inference time, and memory
use. Establishing this compromise is a central concern of the proposed
design.

Deep networks have transformed the accuracy of stereo matching. The most
accurate systems now pair a foundation-scale monocular prior with an
iterative refinement core; FoundationStereo, for example, reports 0.34
pixel end-point error on the SceneFlow benchmark with a backbone of
roughly 340 million parameters \cite{wen2025foundationstereo}. That
accuracy comes at a computational cost that embedded hardware cannot pay.
The edge accelerators found on mobile robots and drones deliver a few
tera-operations per second, carry a few gigabytes of memory, and operate
within a power envelope of a few watts to a few tens of watts. A network
with hundreds of millions of parameters and hundreds of milliseconds of
latency does not fit that budget. The problem this thesis addresses is
therefore not accuracy alone but the joint objective of accuracy, latency,
and memory: a stereo network that is accurate enough to be useful, small
enough to fit in on-device memory, and fast enough to run in real time on
an edge accelerator. Figure \ref{fig:edge_gap} places the published
literature on this trade-off and marks the region this thesis targets.

\section{Research Questions / Problem Statements}
Stereo matching takes a pair of rectified images and produces a dense
disparity map, from which metric depth follows directly through the
calibrated relation between disparity and depth. This thesis addresses
that task under a hardware budget rather than an accuracy budget alone:
a few million parameters, real-time latency on the target device, a
small inference working set, and integer-friendly operators. The design
assumes rectified binocular input, a maximum disparity of 192 pixels at
the inference resolution, and training on synthetic data with dense
ground truth.

Those requirements frame four research questions, each of which the thesis
answers with measured evidence:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{RQ1.} Can a stereo network within a few million parameters,
and inside the sub-3 million edge budget, reach competitive in-domain
accuracy when trained on synthetic data with dense ground truth?
\item \textbf{RQ2.} Does such a compact, synthetic-only network generalize
zero-shot to an unseen real domain, without any target-domain adaptation?
\item \textbf{RQ3.} Can it meet the real-time latency target on the intended
edge device class, after inference optimization and eight-bit deployment?
\item \textbf{RQ4.} Does the design tolerate the small vertical
misalignments of imperfect rectification, the condition a low-cost
stereo rig produces, which is the second objective of this thesis?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Objectives}
The objectives of this thesis are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item To design a computationally efficient stereo matching pipeline,
leveraging AI-based disparity refinement to enhance depth estimation on
resource-limited platforms.
\item To design an architecture able to withstand camera rectification
imperfections.
\end{enumerate}

This thesis addresses passive rectified binocular stereo, trained under
supervision on synthetic data with dense ground truth. The network is
compact, on the order of a few million parameters, and is evaluated both
in domain and zero-shot on an unseen real domain. Its inference is
optimized for the edge latency envelope and checked on real camera pairs.
The recovered disparity is then used for metric three-dimensional
reconstruction: real scenes captured by the project's stereo rig are
back-projected into colored point clouds through the calibrated geometry,
and this is the reconstruction named in the thesis title. Active sensing,
self-supervised training, monocular depth estimation, and the production
of a deployment software development kit fall outside the scope. Evidence
for the second objective, tolerance to imperfect rectification, is
twofold. The first part is a controlled perturbation study on synthetic
data with known ground truth.
The second is a real-data demonstration on a low-cost stereo rig whose
factory rectification is imperfect, where the model still keeps error low.

One might ask why this thesis designs a new compact network instead of
compressing an existing large one. There are two reasons. The accuracy of
foundation-scale systems rests largely on their monocular priors and
their sheer scale, and neither survives aggressive compression, so
compressing such systems tends to remove the very component that makes
them accurate. In addition, the tile-hypothesis and recurrent-refinement
families were built for efficiency from the start and give a cleaner
starting point for an edge budget.

\section{Key Contributions}
The contributions of this thesis are the following:
\begin{itemize}
\item StereoLite, a 2.96 million parameter edge-budget stereo network that
combines a truncated YOLO feature encoder, a left-image recurrent context
stream, plane-aware coarse-to-fine tile refinement, and a fail-soft
quarter-resolution geometry encoding volume.
\item An evidence-gated design methodology that admits an architectural
choice only after it wins a controlled single-knob ablation on a fixed
100-pair harness, where competing variants share data, seed, and step
budget so a measured difference is attributable to the design change alone.
\item A trained checkpoint that reaches 0.78 pixel end-point error and 3.40
percent D1-all on the full FlyingThings3D test set of 4,370 pairs while
running in real time on a laptop RTX 3050 GPU, at 49.8 ms per forward pass
at $384 \times 640$ in half precision.
\item A training pipeline that gives the compact tile-hypothesis model its
cross-domain accuracy, reaching 10.9 percent zero-shot Middlebury 2014
D1-all, within four points of the larger distillation-trained
LiteAnyStereo.
\item An equivalence-proven inference-optimization pass that cuts latency by
a factor of 1.74 with no loss of accuracy and identifies the operators that
block eight-bit deployment, so that the optimized eight-bit model runs in
36.3 ms, or 27.5 frames per second, on the Jetson Orin Nano.
\item A demonstration that the design tolerates imperfect rectification:
under a controlled vertical misalignment sweep, end-point error rises only
from 1.03 to 1.53 px across the sub-pixel to one-pixel range a calibrated
rig produces, and the predicted disparity back-projects into metric colored
point clouds on real indoor scenes.
\item A survey of the efficient and edge-deployable stereo families that
grounds the design choices, stating what edge-deployable stereo requires and
mapping the accuracy, latency, and memory trade-offs of the published
literature.
\end{itemize}

\section{Organization of the Report}
The rest of this report is arranged as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item Chapter~\ref{ch:lit} surveys the stereo-matching literature, moving
from the classical pipeline through the deep-learning eras to the efficient
and foundation-model families. It states what edge-deployable stereo
requires and compares the published methods on accuracy, latency, and memory.
\item Chapter~\ref{ch:design} sets out the engineering design and
methodology of StereoLite. It covers the design requirements and criteria,
the design steps, the controlled ablation methodology, and the
block-by-block architecture together with its assessment metrics.
\item Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation} describes the system development and
implementation: the selection of parameters and components, the training
objective and protocol, the software realization, and the edge-inference
optimization.
\item Chapter~\ref{ch:experimental} covers the experimental setup and data
collection, including the datasets, the stereo rig and calibration, the test
benches, and the measurement procedures.
\item Chapter~\ref{ch:results} presents and discusses the results: the
training dynamics, the ablation studies, the in-domain and zero-shot
accuracy, the rectification robustness, the efficiency and deployment
measurements, and the three-dimensional reconstruction.
\item Chapter~\ref{ch:socio} considers the socio-economic impact of the
work along with its environmental and sustainability aspects.
\item Chapter~\ref{ch:management} records how the thesis was managed, its
time frame, its overall and component-level budget, its resource planning,
and the division of work between the authors.
\item Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} concludes the report, states its
limitations, and outlines future work.
\end{itemize}
The appendices address the knowledge profiles, the complex engineering
problems, and the complex engineering activities engaged by the thesis
(Appendices A to C), followed by the technical specifications, the
plagiarism report, and the AI writing report (Appendices D to F).
